Table~\ref{tab:welfare-comparison} asks a narrower question of the same fifteen economies:
not what happens, but what the happening is worth. Four of its six columns are new readings of
one identical allocation, and their disagreements are the substance of the table.

The first two columns separate the private from the total. $W_C$ values household consumption
alone; $W_D$ the demand bundle including the programme. They never disagree in sign, and in the
flexible-wage columns they are close --- $+0.14$ against $+0.08$ under the standard CGE closure
at $F1$. Where they part company is precisely where the programme is large relative to private
demand: at the wage-pinned pole under external finance they read $+2.26$ against $+3.20$, and the
gap is the programme's own contribution to the bundle rather than to the household's consumption.

The remaining columns are where the table earns its place, because they disagree in
\emph{sign} in thirteen of the fifteen cells. $W_{CES}$ evaluates the bundle at the
\emph{baseline} preference weights, and it is this evaluation that prices the composition shift
the programme imposes: it reports a loss in every $F1$ cell ($-0.25$ to $-0.45$) and in every
$F2$/$F3$ cell except the wage-pinned pole under external finance. $W_{CES^*}$ evaluates the
same bundle at the \emph{adopted} weights, those the household would need in order to have
chosen that basket, and reports a gain everywhere, from $+0.25$ to $+3.56$. Neither column is
an error, and the pair is more informative than either alone: their distance measures the value
of the composition change under the household's original preferences, while their agreement,
where it occurs, marks a verdict that survives the preference convention.

That surviving case is a single one. At the wage-pinned pole under external finance both
readings are positive ($+2.83$ and $+3.56$) --- the same cell in which the programme is
expansionary rather than merely redistributive (Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}). Everywhere else
the programme purchases its output by moving the household off its preferred basket, and whether
that registers as a loss or a gain is decided not by the allocation but by whether one holds the
household's preferences fixed while the basket moves. The leisure-augmented columns damp the
magnitudes --- from $+3.56$ to $+1.59$ at the wage-pinned pole, where the employment gain is
largest and the leisure term therefore bites hardest --- and leave that pattern untouched.

At the headline the matrix describes nine distinct economies rather than fifteen: the
fixed-real-wage and Leontief columns coincide bit-for-bit, and under the two flexible-wage
columns the tax-financed and the externally financed variants do so as well. The demand
impulse is identical in every cell, so what separates the columns is never the size of the
programme but the margin through which the economy is allowed to absorb it --- and the two
axes along which we have organised the closures, the labour market and the financing of the
programme, contribute very unequally to the answer.

\paragraph{The labour margin.} Holding the financing fixed at $F1$, a pure repurposing of
consumption, three observations stand out. First, the standard CGE closure is \emph{exactly}
output-neutral: real GDP and the price level are unchanged to the printed digit in all three
financings. This is what the Hulten-type aggregations lead one to expect, and it deserves to
be said plainly, because it defines the benchmark against which the rest of the table is read:
with one flexible wage and freely mobile labour, a reallocation of demand has no first-order
aggregate output effect whatever, and the programme acts on composition alone. Second, closing
the labour margin at either end breaks that neutrality, and does so in opposite directions.
With immobile labour and sectoral wages the economy absorbs the programme almost entirely
through relative prices: the deflator rises by $+0.63$ per cent while output slips by $-0.01$
per cent. With a sectoral supply response, employment and output rise together instead, by
between $+0.12$ and $+0.19$ per cent over the elasticity bracket we report, and the price
response is correspondingly damped, from $+0.27$ down to $+0.10$ per cent as $\eta_{s,i}$
rises. Third, the wage-pinned closures sit at the opposite end again, and their distinguishing
feature is that they carry no price response at all ($\Delta P = 0.00$): the real-wage peg
makes the consumer price index the numeraire, so adjustment happens entirely on the quantity
side.

Because the elastic-supply responses are monotone in $\eta_{s,i}$ --- output, employment and
consumption rise with it while the price response falls --- we report that column as an
interval rather than at a single elasticity. The bracket $\eta_{s,i}\in[0.5,2]$ spans the
substantive range of answers, with $\eta_{s,i}=0.5$, close to the values usually calibrated for
sectoral labour supply, as the headline point value; the ranking of the columns is unaffected
anywhere inside it.

\paragraph{The financing margin.} The financing of the programme turns out to be the heavier
of the two axes. For every flexible-wage closure, moving from $F1$ to $F2$ or $F3$ turns
consumption welfare from a small non-negative number into a large negative one: the standard
CGE column moves from $+0.14$ to $-1.82$ per cent, the no-reallocation column from $+0.10$ to
$-1.98$ per cent. Repurposing consumption is close to neutral for the household; raising the
same resources through a tax, or borrowing them abroad, is not. The reason is visible in the
accounting rather than in the elasticities: $F2$ and $F3$ both place a claim on household
resources, one through the tax bill and one through the external position, and neither claim is
a component of the consumption bundle whose value the welfare column measures.

\paragraph{Where the two margins meet.} The two axes are not additive, and they interact in
exactly one place --- the most interesting cell of the matrix. At the wage-pinned pole the
direction of the financing effect reverses: with taxes, consumption falls by $1.53$ per cent,
whereas with external finance it \emph{rises} by $2.26$ per cent, with output and employment up
by $1.78$ per cent and the external position improving by $1.33$ per cent of GDP. The mechanism
is the one the accounting of Section~\ref{sec:kernel} makes visible. When the real wage is
pegged, externally financed public demand enlarges the volume of activity without tightening the
household's budget constraint, so the programme expands the economy rather than merely
redistributing within it. The financing wedge is therefore not a property of the programme but
of the wage regime under which it is run: under the standard CGE closure the three financings
are nearly indistinguishable, whereas at the wage-pinned pole they differ by almost four
percentage points of consumption --- and they differ in sign.

Two qualifications belong in the text rather than in a footnote. The exact neutrality of the
standard CGE column is a property of a demand-only shock under constant returns, not of the
programme; what happens when that restriction is relaxed is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}. And the welfare numbers quoted above are the
household-consumption index $\Delta W_C$, which values the private bundle at base preferences.
It is one defensible reading of the same allocation among several. Table~\ref{tab:welfare-comparison}
collects the alternatives, including the demand-based indices that price the composition shift
the programme imposes, and Section~\ref{subsec:robust} compares them.
