\section{From transformation research to input--output models}\label{sec:io-bridge}

Proposals for decarbonisation differ widely in ambition, from modest initiatives that step up existing programmes to large-scale suggestions for a fundamental socio-ecological transformation. What the two ends of this range share is that both imply a repurposing of spending, by private agents, by the public sector, or by an interaction of the two. In that respect such programmes resemble traditional public spending programmes (e.g.\ \citealp{Pollin.2015, Mazzucato.2013}), and they invite the same question: what are the economic consequences of such repurposed or additional spending?

The recent literature exploits this similarity in several, rather different ways. One strand is technocratic in tone: public spending is presented as a functional means, or even a necessary requirement, for decarbonisation and socio-ecological transformation \citep{Mazzucato.2013, Mazzucato.2018}. A second strand is political: on the same basis, public spending is argued to alleviate environmental degradation and social exclusion at once, and thus to serve as a keystone of a just transition \citep{NewellMulvaney.2013}. A third, more analytical strand uses input--output models to identify structural dependencies and critical junctures, and thereby to assess the resilience and versatility of economies in times of intense transitions \citep{Farmer.2024, BF2019, Acemoglu.2012}. 

An input--output model, in its simplest form, records the inter-industry transactions of an economy in a matrix $A$ of input coefficients and relates total output $x$ to final demand $f$ through $x = Ax + f$, hence $x = (I-A)^{-1}f$ \citep{Leontief.1936, leontief1986input, BlairMiller}. The Leontief inverse $(I-A)^{-1}$ is a multiplier in the strict sense: a shift in final demand propagates along the supply chains of the economy, and the inverse returns the output it calls forth. Reading a policy impulse as a shift in $f$ is the traditional application of such a model -- and it is the sectoral analogue of the Keynesian cross \citep{Keynes.1936}, with the household multiplier replaced by an inter-industry one. That analogy also marks its limit. The classical debates about public spending -- the balanced-budget result of \citet{Haavelmo.1945}, who notes that the state, unlike the household, spends all of its income, or the ``crowding out'' of private activity discussed by \citet{BlinderSolow.1973} -- turn on exactly the two questions a plain Leontief application answers by assumption: where the spending comes from, and which resources it releases or absorbs. The basic intuition of synergy can therefore be recovered on a more analytical level -- but only under a specific paradigmatic perspective, taken, more often than not, unconsciously. Which perspective is adequate to a socio-ecological transformation is the object of our comparison, and the reason why a plurality of input--output models, rather than a single one, forms the bridge of this paper. Given their analytical and conceptual advantages, their wide applicability and their resonance with contemporary political challenges, it is no surprise that many applied studies in transformational research use input--output models to assess the potential impacts of such policies \citep{Garrett.2017, Hornykewycz.2025, Markandya.2016, Markaki.2013}.

Our exemplary application goes to the heart of these debates by focusing on the key issue in transformation debates, namely the decarbonization of the residential sector. By taking Germany as an example we focus on a major European economy that is marked by a strong path dependence towards the use of fossil energy sources, especially in the residential sector, but also in energy production, which is key for providing alternative means of provisioning basic housing services. We start from a concrete estimate for additional investment requirements in Germany to arrive at a carbon-neutral residential sector by 2050 \citep{Hornykewycz.2025}. Such an effort would require more than doubling spending on renovation and increase annual expenditures on this by about 58 bn per year on average (in 2023 prices), focused on a small subset of all sectors.\footnote{The following sectors are subject to demand shock (with expected relative change given in brackets): Glass and glassware (+50.3\%), ceramic products and processed stones (+37.3 \%), specialized construction work (+27.8\%), rubber and plastic products (+13.4\%), chemicals and chemical products (+3.7\%), machinery (+1.2\%) and electrical equipment (+1.0\%).} In 2019 prices, the base year of the input--output table, this corresponds to 40.3 bn, or 1.33\% of GDP. We write this programme as a vector $g$ of additional real final demand by sector, $g_i \geq 0$, so that in the plain Leontief reading it simply adds to final demand, $x = (I-A)^{-1}(f+g)$, and costs $\sum_i p_i g_i$ at prices $p$. Who bears this cost is left open in that reading; the financing closures of Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} are three answers to exactly this question. By taking this number -- and the estimated sectoral distribution of spending needs -- as our vantage point, we anchor our illustration in an empirically and politically relevant case that is a valid example for the kind of ``repurposing of existing expenditures'' that a socio-ecological transformation would entail.
