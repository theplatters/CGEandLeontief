\section{A shared machine room: the core model setup}\label{sec:kernel}

All fifteen different constellations captured in the matrix presented in table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5} share one static kernel: a production
network calibrated to an input--output table, cost-minimizing producers and
a utility-maximizing household. This kernel is held fixed by design as it provides a shared analytical skeleton; the closures change only which variables adjust. This section states this core analytical component in its most minimal form; the relevant first-order conditions that emerge from different closure setups are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:closure-focs}.

\subsection{The input--output table}\label{subsec:io-table}

An input--output table records, for $N$ sectors, the deliveries $x_{ui}$ of
good $u$ to sector $i$, final use $z_u$ of good $u$ (household consumption,
government purchases, investment, exports) and the value added $v_i$ of
each sector. Two accounting identities hold for every sector. Along a row,
output equals intermediate and final use; down a column, revenue equals
the cost of intermediate inputs plus value added:
\begin{align}
p_u y_u &= \sum_{i=1}^N p_u x_{ui} + p_u z_u,
\label{eq:io-row}\\
p_i y_i &= \sum_{u=1}^N p_u x_{ui} + v_i.
\label{eq:io-column}
\end{align}
Dividing a column by sector revenue turns the table into cost shares: the
factor share $\alpha_i = v_i/(p_i y_i)$ and the input shares
$\Omega_{iu} = p_u x_{ui}/\sum_{u'} p_{u'}x_{u'i}$ of the intermediate
bundle, with $\sum_u \Omega_{iu} = 1$. Household consumption shares
$\omega_i$ follow in the same way from the final-use columns. Imports and
product taxes on intermediate use are booked as separate leaks
(Appendix~\ref{app:kernel}). The step from the source table to these shares is
documented in Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline}.

The table is a static snapshot of the underlying economy: it fixes shares at one point, is silent about how they respond when prices or demand change. That is the job of the optimization problems discussed below.

\subsection{The optimization problems}\label{subsec:optimization}

On the production side each sector is represented as a single competitive firm with a constant-returns technology $\Phi_i$ in labour and intermediate inputs. Given prices $p$, its wage $w_i$ and output $y_i$, these firms aim to minimize cost:
\begin{equation}
\min_{L_i,\,x_{1i},\ldots,x_{Ni}} \; w_i L_i + \sum_{u=1}^N p_u x_{ui}
\qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad \Phi_i(L_i, x_{1i},\ldots,x_{Ni}; A_i) \geq y_i,
\label{eq:cost-min}
\end{equation}
where $A_i$ is productivity. With a unit cost $c_i$, constant returns imply a total cost
$c_i(p,w_i;A_i)\,y_i$, and perfect competition
equates price and unit cost, $p_i = c_i(p,w_i;A_i)$. Input demands follow
from Shephard's lemma, $L_i = y_i\,\partial c_i/\partial w_i$ and
$x_{ui} = y_i\,\partial c_i/\partial p_u$. 
We take $\Phi_i$ to be a nested CES (elasticity $\epsilon$ between labour and
the intermediate composite, $\theta$ among intermediates). We choose this functional form because it sits comfortably between two paradigmatic conceptions of production -- the inflexible, recipe-based Leontief production and the more adaptive Cobb-Douglas production function where substituiton is governed by marginal productivity. Formally, it can reproduce the respective outcomes at corners solutions (SOURCE).
We set $\epsilon = 0.5$ for the elasticity between labour and the intermediate composite and $\theta = 0.5$ for the elasticity among intermediates, so both nests are gross complements.

On the consumption side, a representative household
maximizes utility given its consumption expenditure $E^h$:
\begin{equation}
\max_{c^h} \; U(c^h)
\qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N p_i c_i^h \leq E^h.
\label{eq:utility-max}
\end{equation}
We again represent $U$ by a CES function with elasticity $\sigma = 0.9$.

We calibrate the weights of the CES functions to the shares of the table
($\alpha_i$, $\Omega_{iu}$, $\omega_i$). With baseline prices normalised to
one, the solutions to~\eqref{eq:cost-min} and~\eqref{eq:utility-max} then
reproduce the table exactly, whatever the elasticities; $\epsilon$, $\theta$
and $\sigma$ matter only once prices move away from the baseline.
In equilibrium, output must meet intermediate and final demand in every
sector: the row identity~\eqref{eq:io-row}, which holds in the table by
construction, becomes a market-clearing condition that prices must restore
after a shock.

These conditions determine prices and quantities only once wages,
employment and household resources are pinned down. The table and the
optimization problems are silent on both: a closure supplies them.

\subsection{The welfare measure}\label{subsec:welfare}

The approach above gives an estimate for \emph{Real GDP} based on inspecting
the wage bill $\sum_i w_i L_i$. For scenarios with price changes the wage bill
is deflated to reflect changes in purchasing power\footnote{Specifcally, we use a seven-component T\"ornqvist price index of final demand (consumption, government purchases and the programme, investment, exports, and the three import and tax leaks booked in Appendix~\ref{app:kernel})}. However, under a demand-only shock every price is pinned at its baseline value in the majority of the scenarios captured by table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5}. In these cases the deflator is exactly one and real GDP moves
with the employment index $w\sum_i L_i$ alone. Hence, in our setup deflation is only required for the no-reallocation and the elastic labor supply closures. 

However, real GDP measures how much is produced, not how well the household fares. In this context, different assumptions on the valuation of this output can be made and  such assumptions often represent different theoretical viewpoints or normative stances. 

A conventional approach for our problem would be to employ a \emph{T\"ornqvist welfare index}, that compares household consumption bundle against its baseline.  In practical terms this measure of welfare will be rather sensitive to the financial closure applied. Specifically, any closure that reallocates spending from the private to the public sector -- i.e., closures F2 and F3 for the scenarios with constrained factor supply and closure F2 for scenarios with flexible quantities -- will mechanically show a negative impact on consumption as expenditures in the public sector do not contribute to welfare per definition. While internally consistent, this feature challenges the validity of the \emph{T\"ornqvist welfare index} for the case of a transformational stimulus, as we would take no harm to assume that public and private spending would supply a quite similar and comparable bundle of goods. Against the backdrop that both types of spending are assumed to lead to the same outcome it is difficult to sustain the assumption that these very same goods and services do not contribute to welfare in case they are booked as public expenditures.

Alternatively, and because the representative household has homothetic CES preferences, the welfare assessemnt could directly honor this assumption by employing an \emph{exact} money-metric assessment of welfare change: with the expenditure function $e(p,u)=u\,\Pi(p)$, the change in real consumption expenditure $E^h/\Pi(p)$ is the consumption-equivalent (equivalent variation) of the shock. Under the consumption-price numeraire $\Pi(p)=1$ --- the CES price index of household consumption, which is always normalized to one --- this measure is simply the change in after-tax, post-saving household resources $E^h$.  It differs from a pure quantity index by taking the imposed utility function seriously.\footnote{A fixed-weight index equals this measure only where the valued bundle grows proportionally, and the size of the gap is set by the distance of the substitution elasticity from one. The two therefore diverge even at pinned prices and unchanged preference weights, as long as the composition of the bundle moves.} While it can be justified as a alternative baseline choice for the valuation algorithm on this basis, it also comes with a built-in status quo bias as the current composition of final consumption is deemed optimal. Also, in its standard form this valuation is still only focused on private expenditure only.

Important variations of these base approaches can be obtained when shifting the definition of what, exactly, is valued by the welfare index. One obvious take here is to introduce a final demand index that captures not only net household consumption but also the spending programme as such, which then undergoes further valuation.\footnote{Public demand is exogenous in this kernel rather than chosen by an optimizing agent, so valuing it at all is a stance and not an implication of the model. We make the stance explicit by letting the valued bundle be $c+\lambda^{G}\,(g^G+g)$, with $\lambda^{G}$ the weight on public relative to private absorption. Throughout we set $\lambda^{G}=1$ --- public spending is valued at cost, as in the national accounts --- and $\lambda^{G}=0$ would return the pure consumption measure as a check.} A second option is to include leisure as a standalone variabel that determines social welfare jointly with economic output. And, indeed, the options above are not only silent on public expenditure but also on the the disutility of the additional work, which matters for all closures where employment absorbs the shock.

Based on these considerations we now turn to presenting the core results of our key application -- a demand shock on the Germany economy focused on renovation of residential housing -- to illustrate the variety of results to be obtained from the closure scenarios introduced in table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5}. When doing so we take the peculiar role of different measure of economic welfare into account to show, how the estimated economic impacts can be translated into, partially competing, welfare assessements.
