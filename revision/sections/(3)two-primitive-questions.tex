\section{Two primitive questions, a simplified matrix and some complications}\label{sec:two-questions}

As already indicated, key points of disjuncture in input-output modeling can be mapped onto classic cleavages separating different traditions of economic thought. Such traditions can often be characterized in terms of the basic analytical ideas -- or ``rivers'' in the history of economic thought (SCREPANIT/ZAMGANI with p.) -- they endorse. Where these basic ideas have a different emphasis or contradict outright, a paradigmatic cleavage is in sight \citep{Dobusch.2012}. Two such classic fields of tension in economic thinking also lie at the heart of different approaches to input-output modeling: the nature of the short run and the role of money and credit, as already captured by Table~\ref{tab:overview}.

%##AG: shal we wed in some closure terms already in this paragraph to prepare tbale 2 – probably yes?
When mapping the rough distinctions from our introductory table more specifically to the realm of input-output models, we find that different labor market closures are the key entry point for different understandings of the short run: if labor is tight or fully utilized, increased monetary demand will increase wages and prices as capacity constraints bind as computable general equilibrium models (CGEs) typically suggest. Conversely, if free factors of production are available, additional expenditures will lead to an expansion of employment and, correspondingly, output. This classic output adjustment closure \citep{Taylor1990} is what one gets from a simple linear extrapolation of the Leontief inverse. Both visions can be unified in one analytical apparatus (see \citealp{Robinson.2006} and Section~\ref{sec:kernel}), just differentiated by different assumptions on what is exogenous or endogenous when monetary expenditures increase. The two poles are the standard CGE closure, in which one flexible wage clears the labour market at full employment and additional demand is met by reallocation at given aggregate output, and the fixed real wage closure, in which the pegged wage pins prices so that employment and output absorb demand; its fixed-coefficient corner, the Leontief closure, reproduces the Leontief-inverse multiplier exactly. While the former is the obvious neoclassical standard model, the latter reflects the intuition of a multiplier and a notion of economic planning that shows greater resonance with (Post-)Keynesian, radical and evolutionary approaches (SOURCES????).

Financial closures on the other hand encode prior beliefs about the role of our monetary system and the nature of money. As input-output models are typically conceived as static, operating outside of time (see however AUSNAHMEN ZITIEREN??), there is no borrowing against the future -- additional expenditure cannot be externally financed in the conventional sense, but has to be repaid within the same period (see however AUSNAHMEN ZITIEREN??). Hence, while a traditional, simplified application of a Leontief inverse would suggest some external stimulus, exactly such an external force is at odds with the internal consistency of the model. In other words, canonical input-output models implicitly build on an exogenous view on money and restrict the application of ideas on endogenous money creation as it lacks a any framework to conceptualizethe intertemporal role of debt.

While the crosswise recombination of these different options on closure choices already gives rise to some complexity, the underlying choice menu is nonetheless somewhat rough and -- in the case of the short-run -- simplistically dichotomous. Hence, it comes as no surprise that further established variations of closure setups exist that try to address this point. An obvious starting point from a mainstream perspective to introduce greater variety in labor market outcomes is by explicitly referring to a leisure-income trade-off, which leads to the canonical \textit{elastic labor supply model}: Assuming that the substitution effect dominates, higher wages will attract additional labor so that capacity constraints do not bite with full force. In the \emph{elastic labour supply} closure the fixed labour endowment is replaced by a sector-specific labor supply curve, $L_i^{s} = \bar L_i\,[\,(w_i/\Pi(p))/(w_i^{0}/\Pi^{0})\,]^{\eta_s}$, with one common elasticity $\eta_s$. Two changes of functional form follow: labour supply becomes \emph{behavioural} rather than a quantity restriction or a price pin, and the single aggregate labour market becomes $N$ sectoral markets with $N$ wages.\footnote{The latter assumption is not strictly necessary, but relevant for our case of application which typically implies shocks with rather heterogenous impact on sectors.}

While this modification brings greater responsiveness on the side of employment other variations in the mainstream literature assume forms of rigidity that go even beyond the conventional CGE labor market closure. A prominent recent example is given by \citet{BF2019}, who introduce a ``no-reallocation'' case as a baseline scenario to articulate the view that sectoral productivity shocks might impact on aggregate outcomes in a non-linear way. Relative to Table~\ref{tab:closure-conditions} this is not a column but
a more rigid, sectoral extension of the labour axis: it projects the standard CGE
column's aggregate restriction on a sectoral level, i.e., $\sum_i L_i = \bar L$ (fixed employment levels for all sectors, so ``no-reallocation'') and correspindingly relies on sectorally flexible wage $w_i$. Formally, the transition from the standard CGE model to the no-reallocation model is governed on the production side, where $\eta$ represent the mobility of labor across sectors, whith $\eta=0$ for the no-reallocation case.

In the context of our case of application -- a socio-ecological transformation that is tied to sector-specific shifts or injections in final demand -- such a baseline setup is especially pessimistic as it assumes labor market rigidity across sectors. As an effect, sectors with strong demand shocks are then hopelessly overwhelmed as workers from other sectors cannot ``reallocate'' to where capacity is needed. This result, however, resonates with intuition that additional rigidities on labor markets will complicate transformation efforts building on exactly such a reallocation of labor.\footnote{In this case assumptions deviate from the neoclassical baseline, but strengthen the general laissez-faire policy stance associated with its standard closure. Also note that related work on rigidities in capital or financial markets also exists, but is beyond the scope of this paper (see, however, (SOME SOURCES))}

Technically the \emph{Leontief closure} named here can be represented with the very same analytical apparatus as the conventional CGE model: with substitution elasticities taken to their Leontief limits, fully flexible production factors and the real wage pegged \eqref{eq:closure-delta}, prices then follow from constant costs and final demand determines output. This is the corner most applied transformation studies work with, usually without emphasis. Given this restrictive setup the \emph{Leontief closure} can itself be conceived as corner solution of a broader set of related approaches that retain the basic idea that ``quantities adjust'' without also committing to a Leontief production function. In their basic form these \textit{fixed (real) wage} models assume constant real wages (AG: EQUATION HERE), which pins down priced and leaves the quantity margin free. They stand in the same tradition as the Leontief inverse: it is the (more general) closure through which the Keynesian multiplier intuition enters the kernel (see also section \ref{sec:kernel}).

Observing these additional models and corresponding closure variations moves our view on the short run from the manicheistic scenario formulated in table \ref{tab:closure-conditions} to a more nuanced exposition of different conceptions of the short run: the \textit{no-reallocation approach} imposes additional rigidity on labor markets, \textit{elastic labor supply models} allow for introducing behavioral responses and the \textit{fixed real wage models} try to generalize the scope of the output adjistment view. Moreover, the first two of these approaches are articulated on a sectoral level and the latter can be extended to one. In what follows we suggest to take the now resulting portfolio of closure choices as means to illustrate the consequences of different closure assumption -- and, hence, paradigmatic perspectives -- on results when study the impacts of a socio-ecological transformation ny means of input-output models.

Before we map these five closures in a monumental table further below (REF!) it makes sense to point out the sometimes surprising overlaps that arise for certain characteristic parameter values as summarized by Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} below. For one, the no-reallocation model differs from the conventional CGE model only by exactly this ``no-reallocation'' assumption, wherese choosing a reallocation term $\eta=1$ instead of $\eta=0$ wold lead to full convergence with the conventional case (as the sectoral level ceases to matter with ``full reallocation''). For another, the elastic labor supply model -- being a curve of solutions rather than a simple corner -- is able to mimic the outcome of the no-reallocation model by assuming that labor market participants lack any flexibility (by setting $eta_s=0$), as well as that of the \textit{fixed real age} model by taking the inverse position (letting $eta_s \rightarrow \inf$). Hence, the elastic labor supply model can be said to sit at the centre of the interparadigmatic bridge between neoclassical interpretations based on factor scarcity and more Keynesian multiplier-style interpretations based on extrapolation via the Leontief inverse \citep{Robinson.2006, Taylor1990}.\footnote{Also, the elastic labor supply model collapses to te conventional CGE in the absence of sectoral heterogeneity.} Finally, as already indicated, the \textit{fixed real wage} closure is equivalent to the classic Leontief result, when production structures are assumed to be rigid. A more detailed exposition of how these overlaps between different closures occur is found in APPENDIX A.

Based on this core observation that a series of, partially overlapping, key axiomatic variations \citep{Kapeller.2013} exist when it comes to closure choice in input-outout modeling we aim to map the basic framework as sketched in Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} to an applied level by asking: which of these assumption will typically make what difference for estimating the economic effects of transformational policies. Moreover, as estimates for such economic effects often serve as a baseline for and precondition for estimating further ecological effects and also play a key role in informing political decision-making, the relevance of this question reaches far beyond the mere technical level. Following this agenda we combine the estimate of investment requirements introduced in section \ref{sec:io-bridge} across different model specifications to better reflect on how the the conceptual distinctions introduced above matter for applied analysis and how different closure dimension (here: labor markets and financing) interact in practice.
