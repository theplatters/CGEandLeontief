# Computable general equilibrium models

Computable general equilibrium (CGE) models are numerical general-equilibrium models in which all markets clear jointly; the standard characterization in the applied literature is "a general-equilibrium model is one in which all markets clear in equilibrium", with only relative prices mattering.[^1] They extend Leontief's empirical, fixed-coefficient Walrasian input–output models by adding substitution in production and demand and by allowing more than one consumer, and they are used to appraise tax, tariff and other policy changes by specifying production and demand parameters and using data reflective of a real economy.[^1]

A canonical CGE can be formulated as a complementarity problem, and open-source extended mathematical programming frameworks such as CIM-EARTH exist for generating and solving it automatically.[^2] The field is covered by standard texts such as Burfisher (2016) and Hosoe et al. (2010), and typical applications include economic integration, eco-taxes and environmental problems, regulatory reform, taxation reform and transport-system planning.[^3][^4]

Within this project, the Beyond Hulten paper compares CGE models with input–output analysis: the IO multiplier model is the special case of a CGE with fixed prices and unconstrained factor supply, so the labour-supply elasticity is the parameter that moves the model between the full-employment CGE endpoint and the IO multiplier endpoint.[^5][^6] Because the equilibrium is Walrasian, Walras' law can make one price/cost equation redundant, but only when the household budget constraint is actually enforced — a point that matters for the mobile-labour implementation.[^7]

The project's CGE reading list records the intended progressive study of the field — from the Shoven & Whalley survey through textbooks to reduced IFPRI-style and GTAP-type models — as its desired outcome.[^8]
