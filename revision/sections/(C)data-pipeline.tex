\section{From the input--output table to $\Omega$: the data pipeline}\label{app:pipeline}

One published table goes in; one set of shares comes out. Every closure in the
paper is calibrated on the same objects --- the input shares $\Omega$, the
composite factor share $\alpha$, the Domar weights $\lambda$ and the household
consumption shares $\beta$ --- so differences in results cannot originate in the
data step. The accounting built on them is in Section~\ref{app:abill}.

\subsection{Source data}\label{app:pipeline-source}

The table is Germany's input--output account for 2019 (revision 2019), 71
product groups at basic prices. Rows $1$--$71$ are the modeled sectors; row $72$ (households' own goods and services) lies outside the modeled block; rows $73$--$75$ represent the domestic intermediate bill $A$, the imported intermediates $m_i$ and the product taxes less subsidies $t_i$, both by using sector; rows $77$--$84$ carry value added and output. Columns $2$--$72$ are the 71 using sectors, columns $75$--$81$ the seven final-demand categories (household consumption, government, investment,
inventories, exports).

\subsection{The intermediate-use matrix}\label{app:pipeline-z}

The $71\times71$ sector block is the matrix of domestic deliveries $Z$, with
$x_{ui}$ the value supplied by $u$ to $i$ at basic prices; its row and column sums
reproduce \eqref{eq:io-row}--\eqref{eq:io-column} once the imported and taxed
content is added. That content sits outside the block and is reported only by
\emph{using} sector: row $73$ is the domestic intermediate bill $A_i$, row $74$
the imported intermediates $m_i$ and row $75$ the product taxes $t_i$. Because the
source reports imports by user and not by supplying sector, the model needs no
domestic/import split of $Z$ at all: it charges the domestic bill and books rows
$74$--$75$ as the explicit leaks of Section~\ref{sec:kernel}.

\subsection{The A-bill and the external account}\label{app:abill}

The intermediate use of a sector splits into three recorded components: the
domestic bill $A_u$, imported intermediates $M_{\mathrm{int},u}$ and product
taxes on intermediate use $T_{\mathrm{int},u}$. Together they exhaust gross
output once the value-added bill is deducted, since gross output is value added
plus intermediate consumption and the $\lambda_u$ are measured in baseline
income units with GDP $=1$. The kernel charges the domestic component as input
demand and books the other two as the explicit leaks $m_i$ and $t_i$ of
Section~\ref{sec:kernel}, which is what makes the row identity \eqref{eq:io-row}
close sector by sector. The accounting closes on both sides: the bill identity
is checked against the raw table in Section~\ref{app:pipeline-checks}, and the
external account is the identity \eqref{eq:financing-external-account} of
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Normalizing to cost shares: $\Omega$}\label{app:pipeline-omega}

Dividing each column of $Z$ by the user's intermediate bill and transposing
gives the cost shares
$\Omega_{iu}=p_ux_{ui}/\textstyle\sum_{u'}p_{u'}x_{u'i}$, every row summing to one
by construction --- the foundation the CES price index relies on. Because $Z$
holds domestic deliveries, $\Omega$ \emph{is} the cost structure the model prices
(the kernel stores it as $\Omega^{\mathrm{raw}}$); the imported and taxed content
of the intermediate bill never enters it but is charged as the explicit leaks
$M_{\mathrm{int}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{int}}$ (Section~\ref{app:abill}).

The matrix is sparse: $1{,}631$ of its
$5{,}041$ entries ($32.4\%$) are exactly zero and half lie below $10^{-3}$.
The self-supply diagonal averages $0.224$ (median $0.201$) and is strongly
skewed --- IT and information services $0.828$, pig iron and steel $0.724$,
non-ferrous metals $0.671$, legal and management consultancy $0.569$ --- with
seven sectors above $0.45$ (13, 18, 19, 48, 53, 58, 68). Off the diagonal the largest
entries are water transport's purchase of warehousing and transport services
($0.543$) and insurance's from finance-related services ($0.448$). Normalization
runs down the using sector, so
columns are not normalized: summed over users, a supplier's column ranges from
$0.031$ to $4.03$ and averages $1.00$, as both margins must.

\subsection{Value added, factor shares and Domar weights}\label{app:pipeline-va}

Value added is separated in four components with no leakage: compensation of employees
$1{,}763{,}874$~EUR~m, consumption of fixed capital $609{,}861$, net operating
surplus $656{,}413$ and other net taxes on production $-2{,}330$ --- gross

the income weights $\lambda_i\alpha_i$ sum to exactly one.\par}

\subsection{Final demand and household shares $\beta$}\label{app:pipeline-fd}

The seven final-demand columns form four blocks: household consumption
($1{,}309{,}448$~EUR~m), private nonprofit institutions ($49{,}237$),
government ($648{,}260$), investment --- equipment ($240{,}444$), construction
($280{,}975$), inventories ($-29{,}801$) --- and exports ($1{,}277{,}574$). These
are the domestic components and sum to the $3{,}776{,}137$ of
Section~\ref{app:pipeline-checks}. Imported and taxed final use is reported by
category, not by supplying sector, so each category's total is split
proportionally into a domestic part and its import and product-tax components;
a non-positive category takes no domestic content.

The household shares $\beta_u$ are calibrated so that the model clears at the
baseline $y=\lambda$. The residual of the domestic-bill accounting,
\begin{equation}
c^{\mathrm{dom}}_u = \lambda_u - \sum_i \Omega_{iu} A_{\mathrm{bill},i}
  - (1-m_u)\,(g^G_u + \mathrm{inv}_u) - x_u ,
\label{eq:betaresid}
\end{equation}
is by the table's row identity the observed domestic household final demand, so
nothing is clamped beyond floating-point dust. The observable and the model's
share sit on different bases: the residual is domestic demand at basic prices,
whereas $\beta$ is a share of \emph{gross} household demand at purchasers'
prices. The block is therefore grossed up,
$c^{\mathrm{gross}}_u = c^{\mathrm{dom}}_u/(1-m_u)$, before the weights are
normalized to one. $\beta$ is concentrated --- real estate services $0.189$, retail trade $0.088$, food $0.059$, accommodation and food services $0.050$. The saving rate is not assumed but the data-implied residual
$s=1-\sum_u c_u/(1-\sum_u g_u)=0.1199$, with government at $0.2141$ of baseline
income, exports $0.4219$, investment $0.1624$ and intermediate imports $0.2214$
(Section~\ref{app:abill}).

\subsection{The published table and its consistency checks}\label{app:pipeline-checks}

Production and income agree exactly, both $3{,}027{,}818$~EUR~m (difference
$0.0$). On the expenditure side two totals must not be confused. The seven
final-use columns sum to $4{,}694{,}897$~EUR~m at purchasers' prices; restricted
to domestic deliveries, rows $1$--$71$, the same columns give
$3{,}776{,}137$~EUR~m at basic prices, and the difference is the recorded final
import content ($656{,}669$) and final product taxes ($254{,}744$) plus the
table's own column gap of $7{,}347$~EUR~m:
$3{,}776{,}137 + 656{,}669 + 254{,}744 + 7{,}347 = 4{,}694{,}897$. Netting the two
margins out of the basic-price total leaves an expenditure measure of GDP of
$2{,}864{,}724$ against the $3{,}027{,}818$ the production side reports, a gap of
$5.387\%$.

That gap is a documented open item of the raw table itself, not a calibration
error of ours: the final-demand columns do not close the basic-price identity.
It is not the $7{,}347$ column gap either --- that is the commodity account
failing to balance column by column, whereas the $5.387\%$ is the distance
between the production and the expenditure measure of GDP.

Table~\ref{tab:io-summary} collects the aggregates these checks refer to, and is
the calibration's starting point before the shares: gross output with its
intermediate, import, tax and value-added components, the final-use block with
its own import and tax margins, and the three sides of GDP. It is assembled
directly from the published table, by label lookup, and the recipe is recorded
in the source of the table file.

\subsection{The composition of the program's spending}\label{app:pipeline-composition}

Two decompositions answer where the program's spending goes, and they must not
be conflated, because they have different bases. The first is the bill itself.
The program is a final-demand vector at purchasers' prices; the table reports
imported final use by final-demand category rather than by product, so we apply
the import and tax shares of the equipment and building columns to it:
$17.8\%$ of the bill is imported final use, $8.7\%$ product taxes, and $73.5\%$
demand for domestic production. The second is what that demand sets in motion:
the induced gross output decomposes into domestic intermediates ($43.5\%$),
imported intermediates ($13.0\%$), product taxes on intermediate use ($0.6\%$)
and value added ($42.9\%$), an identity that holds sector by sector. Per euro of
program spending, imports come to about $0.35$ --- $0.18$ of them directly and
$0.17$ along the supply chains --- product taxes to $0.10$ and the value added
generated to $0.56$, that is $56\%$ of the program's size.

Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-composition} collects both decompositions, with these
per-euro totals in panel~C, which are not shares of a single base. One assumption runs through the figures: the split of the bill into domestic, imported and taxed components. Taking it from the program-weighted import intensity of the sectors the program buys from, instead
of from the category split of the investment columns, lowers total import
content to $31.2\%$, so the honest reading is a band of roughly $31$ to $35\%$ of
the program leaving the economy as imports.

Induced household demand is not included, because the generated imports then would depend on the resulting consumption multiplier and typically increase with it. Second, they use the
cost-side coefficients in supply orientation,
$\Omega^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(A_{\mathrm{bill}}/\lambda)$, the matrix the
kernel inverts; inverting the transposed system instead raises the induced output
to $70{,}466$~EUR~m --- $1.75$ euro per euro of spending against $1.30$ --- and
the imported intermediates to $21.2\%$ of the program against $16.9\%$. Third, the table's own residual:
panel~B closes sector by sector, the table as a whole does not, so the composition
is stated per euro of spending rather than as a closed budget identity
(Section~\ref{app:pipeline-checks}). What it quantifies is what the closed-economy
exposition of Section~\ref{sec:io-bridge} conflates --- imported final use, product taxes and
domestic deliveries --- and it is the content counterpart to the financing accounts
of Section~\ref{sec:application}, where $\Delta D = F + B_{\mathrm{gov}}$ answers
who finances the program.

One reading to avoid: a multiplier read off the model's full response would not be comparable
with these cost shares. Its numerator grows with the response the closure permits --- nearly
three times the program in the third financing regime --- so the ratio can exceed the
program's own import content without that content having changed. It ranks closures by how
much output they let through, not by how import-intensive the spending is.
