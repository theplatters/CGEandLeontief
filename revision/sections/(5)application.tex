\subsection{Aggregate results}\label{subsec:aggregate}

The main results for applying the demand stimulus as described in section \ref{sec:io-bridge} to a set of model economies as conceptualized in sections \ref{sec:two-questions} and \ref{sec:kernel} are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} below. This table now asks a more direct question on the fifteen cells captured in our exposition: not what happens on a level of formalities, but what the happening is worth in terms of economic implications.

We immediately observe that the overall output response to the demand shock for the core neoclassical models in the two leftmost columns is close to zero and that, as already indicated, only the no-reallocation is able to generate price reactions for the simple one-factor model under consideration. And indeed, the exact neutrality of the standard CGE column is a property of a demand-only shock under constant returns.

The response of GDP in the elastic labor supply also remains marginal relative to the size of the stimulus ($+0.12\%$ to $+0.19\%$ vs. $+1.33\%$ in stimulus size), but still paints a slightly more flexible picture of labor market conditions.\footnote{Because the elastic-supply responses are a monotone in $\eta_{s,i}$ --- output, employment and consumption rise with it while the price response falls --- we report that column as an interval rather than at a single elasticity.} The economy provides external finance in most of the flexible wage scenarios, where the genuinely minimal effect is amplified for the no-reallocation scenario due to higher prices for exports and investment. Moreover, for the latter two financing closures we actually observe the same reaction for these three models and two rows collapse into one. These collapsed results are accompanied by a large drop in estimated welfare $W_C$, which is derived from a direct application of the standard \emph{T\"ornqvist welfare index} as described in section \ref{sec:kernel}. This latter observation implies that the differences in $\Delta W_C$ across these scenarios are largely accounting mechanics: private expenditures in F1 are considered relevant for welfare, public expenditures not -- an assumption that will be relaxed further below.

Another simplifying collapse we observe in this assessment is that the fixed real wage model and the Leontief closure do behave identically in the chosen setup as under a demand-only shock: with a fixed wage different substitution elasticities have no means to impact the outcome via relative prices. Results for financing closures reflect a Haavelmo pattern where spent taxes outweigh absorbed income in F2 relative to F1. Nonetheless, in these cases the projections do not differ markedly from the neoclassical core model with only slight changes in GDP predicted. This stands in contrast to the more voluminous expansion observed in the case of financing closure F3, where the full programme (of $1.33\%$ of GDP) is financed externally. The stark differences in welfare estimates can be explained with recourse to the already mentioned private-public distinction (which drives much of the differences between F1 and F2) and the specific role of F3, where externally financed expenditures create additional income (equal to GDP growth), while higher GDP also implies a reduction of the net tax rate, which gives an additional boost on $W_C$ in this scenario.

In sum, we find that the matrix describes nine distinct economies rather than fifteen: the fixed-real-wage and Leontief columns coincide bit-for-bit, and under the two flexible-wage columns the tax-financed and the externally financed variants do so as well. The demand impulse is identical in every cell, so what separates the columns is never the size of the programme but the margin through which the economy is allowed to absorb it --- and the two axes along which we have organised the closures, the labour market and the financing of the programme, contribute unequally to the answer. The outcomes also underscore our main point, namely that closure choices on factor markets and financing conditions matter greatly for studying the economic impacts of transformation effort. GDP responses range from being slightly negative due to allocation frictions in the no-reallocation scenario to a multiplier driven expansion in the externally financed fixed real wage cases.

In addition to these observations on the importance of closure choice we have also seen that welfare evaluations can show some differences to GDP estimates. While the latter are typically of greater interest in applied transformation research employing input-output modeling precisely because they avoid such an additional step of valuation, it is helpful to indicate the diverse paths by which our economic findings can be mapped unto social welfare. This is so because a scenario of an economic repurposing or stimulus in the spirit of a socio-ecological transformation invites us to explicitly consider questions of valuation that potentially underpin specific transformation efforts.

To this end table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison} below reweighs the economic outcomes shown in different scenarios according to a greater variety of welfare measures, starting from a reproduction of the T\"ornqvist $W_C$ in the first column. This measure is then complemented first by $W_D$ which explicitly adds the investment programme content to the relevant consumption basket to signify the fact that private actors are the final recipients of said expenditures. Moving to this measure rectifies the gap to GDP change for the flexible wage models and aligns outcomes across financing closures. Given the nature of transformational policy proposals involving such repurposing or stimulus $W_D$ is arguably a more valid candidate for welfare assessment, at least when assuming that citizens share some pro-environmental preferences.

In addition to that table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison} shows several measures taking the explicit CES-specifications of the utility functions into account, thereby assuming that the substitution elasticity $\sigma=0.9$. The first one of these $W_{CES}$ rationalizes the new bundle of final demand -- that is, consumption plus the spending programme as already in $W_D$ -- in terms of the preferences associated with the original bundle. Hence, the change here captures the welfare impact if we assume that the existing optimal allocation of consumer spending is disturbed by the stimulus (or the repurposing) and, hence, induces a welfare loss. This is also the reason why $W_{CES}$ gives comparatively skeptical evaluations of the program across the board.

The alternative specification shown, $W_{CES^*}$, in contrast assumes that the associated repurposing of spending is actually embraced by the population and that the new equilibrium is internalized. Such an assumption would come quite naturally for all scenarios with financing closure F1, which literally models a repurposing of spending by private actors. And while it might come less naturally for other scenarios the principal claim that some public effort is required to enable citizens to live up to their pro-environmental preference (BRAND; BRAGANZA+KAPELLER) does not ride without evidence. In formal terms, we define $W_{CES^*}$ to compare the value of the new bundle with updated preferences to the value of the old bundle at now dated preferences – a procedure that leads to the most optimistic welfare projections of the variants presented in table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison}.

The final row of welfare estimate shown in Table~\ref{tab:welfare-comparison} adds the labor side to the valuation. Every measure up to this point values the bundle alone; the final row showing $W_L$ now also accounts for the additional work that a programme requires -- so that a gain paid for in additional hours of labor no longer counts as a free gain. The leisure costs imposed by additional employment (or the benefits accruing from its reduction) are extrapolated from the paid wage whereas the remaining setup follows the one imposed for $W_{CES^*}$. Hence, we observe the same values for $W_L$ as we did for $W_{CES^*}$ in the first two scenarios, where employment remains effectively unchanged. For the remaining scenarios it lies mostly below $W_{CES^*}$ mimicking the expansion of employment typically observed thereby strongly discounting welfare gains especially for those scenarios that show the strongest expansion in employment. The one exception to these pattern are the two fixed wage sceanrios under financial closure F1, where employment slightly decreases, which is captured as a welfare benefit by $W_L$, but remains unseen by $W_{CES^*}$.

In sum, while a focus on GDP seems apt and adequate for many purposes in research aiming to connect input-output modeling and concerns for a socio-ecological transformation it has to be acknowledged that such concerns often have a behavioral dimension that is difficult to explicitly recognize in applied work. In this spirit our short exposition served to illustrate how specific welfare measures might deliver misleading results when it comes to assessing transformation policy efforts. Moreover, it also indicates which variations of measuring welfare could be helpful for researchers to make underlying assumptions on behavioral aspects explicit or to formulate potential scenarios for behavioral responses to a given policy.
