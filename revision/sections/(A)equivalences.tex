\section{Equivalences and overlaps of the closure matrix}\label{app:equivalences}

\noindent
Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} observes that several of the five
labour-market closures coincide at characteristic parameter values and
that, under the demand-only shock of our application, several cells of the
evaluation matrix describe the same economy. This appendix states the
coincidences, the mechanism behind each, its logical status --- an exact
identity, a limit, or a definitional inclusion --- and what it leaves
unidentified. The derivations are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}; only the
mechanism is kept here.

\subsection{Why the overlaps arise}\label{app:eqwhy}

Three features of the kernel account for every coincidence below.

\emph{A demand-only shock.} The programme enters final demand at baseline
technology ($A\equiv\one$), so the shock touches neither the price nor the
technology margin. This is the demand-only slice on which the whole matrix
is evaluated; it is the case of our application.

\emph{A price block that cannot see demand.} The zero-profit conditions are
homogeneous of degree one in $(p,w)$ and contain no demand variable, so
relative prices are pinned by technology alone (Lemma~\ref{lem:price}).
Under the consumption-price numeraire the real wage then sits at its
baseline anchor $w/\Pi(p)=1$ for every demand vector, every financing
closure and every shock size (Corollary~\ref{cor:realwage}). A closure
whose only task is to move the \emph{wage} margin is thus inert: the margin
it is meant to clear is already at rest.

\emph{A quantity block that is elasticity-free.} At those prices the
clearing system is exactly affine and independent of $(\theta,\epsilon,
\sigma)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:affine}) --- the production side of the Samuelson
nonsubstitution theorem (Remark~\ref{rem:samuelson}). Every closure that
pegs the real wage therefore solves one and the same linear system,
whatever the technology.

The overlaps are properties of the experiment, not numerical accidents: a
demand-only programme in a one-factor constant-returns economy cannot
identify any elasticity of the kernel.

\subsection{The coincidences}\label{app:eqpairs}

\paragraph{Standard CGE $\equiv$ single-wage elastic supply.}
The supply equation $\sum_i L_i=\Lbar\,(w/\Pi)^{\eta_s}$ and full
employment $\sum_i L_i=\Lbar$ are the same equation once the real-wage
anchor binds: for \emph{every} $\eta_s$, and under every financing closure,
the two systems coincide equation by equation (Proposition~\ref{prop:ab}).
The single-wage supply elasticity $\eta_s$ is therefore unidentified on the
demand-only slice. The coincidence is \emph{derivable} from the anchor; it
is not definitional.

\paragraph{Fixed real wage $\equiv$ Leontief.}
With the real wage pegged, $(p,y)=(\one,y^{\ast})$ solves the system at
every elasticity triple, and the Leontief corner selects the same candidate
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gd}). The equivalence is exact --- not an
$\epsilon\to0$ artefact --- because under a demand-only shock a fixed wage
already pins prices: the substitution elasticities $(\theta,\epsilon,
\sigma)$ have nothing left to move. In the fixed-wage gauge they are
unidentified. The coincidence is definitional in \emph{form} (the Leontief
corner is the fixed-wage closure evaluated at the zero-substitution limit)
but substantive in mechanism (the affine quantity block).

\paragraph{No reallocation $\equiv$ the rigid corner of the sectoral
family.} A frozen allocation and an infinitely inelastic supply curve are
the same row of algebra: setting $\eta_{s,i}=0$ for all $i$ reproduces the
$\eta=0$ endpoint (Proposition~\ref{prop:nest}). This one is exact by
construction and of a different kind from the two above --- it equates two
\emph{closures} rather than two executed cells, and it is why no separate
rigid sectoral cell is reported.

\paragraph{The financing column.} In the flexible-wage rows the transfer
$R$ is exactly the instrument that keeps the labour pin satisfied, and the
two additive financings differ by a shift in $R$ that leaves the real
allocation untouched: $R_{F3}=R_{F2}-B_{\mathrm{gov}}$, so F2 and F3
coincide and the financing label is unidentified
(Section~\ref{sec:gauge}). The fixed-wage rows are the exception: there $R$
is pinned at zero and the labour pin is absent, so financing \emph{does}
bite --- the F2/F3 spread in the fixed-wage row is the one difference the
financing column carries.

\subsection{One family, two rigidities}\label{app:eqfamily}

The elastic closure is a curve rather than a point, and it connects the two
rigidities. At $\eta_s\to0$ the allocation is frozen and the closure is the
no-reallocation case; at $\eta_s\to\infty$ the supply slope diverges and it
approaches the fixed real wage --- a limit, never an attained cell. The
standard CGE closure is the zero-elasticity limit of the \emph{aggregate}
(single-wage) variant of the same family. The two limits differ in what the
vanishing elasticity freezes --- the sectoral allocation or only the total
--- which is why the family reaches both rigidities without coinciding with
either.

This is also where the paper's variety lives. With $N$ sectoral wages the
price block is no longer homogeneous: demand reaches prices through the
wage structure, and the sectoral rows are distinct economies rather than
further copies of a single one (Section~\ref{sec:sectoral}).

\subsection{What is identified, and what is not}\label{app:eqclass}

Table~\ref{tab:equiv-class} collects the coincidences with their status and
what each leaves unidentified. The first three entries are not numerical
accidents: they hold at machine precision in the executed generation, and
none is a mis-specification. They are properties of the \emph{experiment}
--- a demand-only programme in a one-factor constant-returns economy cannot
identify any elasticity of the kernel (the mechanism is
Remark~\ref{rem:samuelson}).

The counting consequence is worth stating. The fifteen cells of
Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} describe \emph{seven} distinct economies when
the single-wage form of the elastic-supply closure is on display (the
elastic-supply row then coincides with the standard CGE row) and
\emph{nine} when the sectoral form is: the gap of two is exactly the
variety claim of Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}. The fixed-wage /
Leontief pair and the flexible-row F2/F3 pair account for the remaining
collapses in both readings.

\subsection{Scope}\label{app:eqscope}

Every statement above is bounded by three conditions: the shock is
demand-only; the first coincidence uses the \emph{single-wage} form; and the
overlap concerns the price margin, since employment still adjusts under the
fixed-wage and Leontief closures.

Off the slice the picture changes. A technology shock ($A\neq\one$) breaks
the real-wage anchor and separates the elastic-supply from the standard CGE
closure, so $\eta_s$ becomes identifiable (Remark~\ref{rem:supply}). And no
rule that writes the \emph{nominal} wage as a function of endogenous
aggregates --- an aggregate wage curve, indexation, a re-anchored numeraire
--- can create demand-sensitive real outcomes within the one-factor price
block (Corollary~\ref{cor:nogo}). The doors through which the demand-only
matrix does acquire variety are catalogued in Section~\ref{sec:scope} of
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} and exploited in
Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}.
