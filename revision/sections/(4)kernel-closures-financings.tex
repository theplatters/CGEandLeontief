\section{A shared machine room: the core model setup}\label{sec:kernel}

% TODO: verbal model overview BEFORE the equations (R1.2).

All fifteen different constellations captured in the matrix presented in table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5} share one static kernel: a production
network calibrated to an input--output table, cost-minimizing producers and
a utility-maximizing household. This kernel is held fixed by design as it provides a shared analytical skeleton; the closures change only which variables adjust. This section states this core analytical component in its most minimal form; the relevant first-order conditions that emerge from different closure setups are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:closure-focs}.

\subsection{The input--output table}\label{subsec:io-table}

An input--output table records, for $N$ sectors, the deliveries $x_{ui}$ of
good $u$ to sector $i$, final use $z_u$ of good $u$ (household consumption,
government purchases, investment, exports) and the value added $v_i$ of
each sector. Two accounting identities hold for every sector. Along a row,
output equals intermediate and final use; down a column, revenue equals
the cost of intermediate inputs plus value added:
\begin{align}
p_u y_u &= \sum_{i=1}^N p_u x_{ui} + p_u z_u,
\label{eq:io-row}\\
p_i y_i &= \sum_{u=1}^N p_u x_{ui} + v_i.
\label{eq:io-column}
\end{align}
Dividing a column by sector revenue turns the table into cost shares: the
factor share $\alpha_i = v_i/(p_i y_i)$ and the input shares
$\Omega_{iu} = p_u x_{ui}/\sum_{u'} p_{u'}x_{u'i}$ of the intermediate
bundle, with $\sum_u \Omega_{iu} = 1$. Household consumption shares
$\omega_i$ follow in the same way from the final-use columns. Imports and
product taxes on intermediate use are booked as separate leaks
(Appendix~\ref{app:kernel}).

The table is a static snapshot of the underlying economy: it fixes shares at one point, is silent about how they respond when prices or demand change. That is the job of the optimization problems discussed below.

\subsection{The optimization problems}\label{subsec:optimization}

On the production side each sector is represented as a single competitive firm with a constant-returns technology $\Phi_i$ in labour and intermediate inputs. Given prices $p$, its wage $w_i$ and output $y_i$, these firms aim to minimize cost:
\begin{equation}
\min_{L_i,\,x_{1i},\ldots,x_{Ni}} \; w_i L_i + \sum_{u=1}^N p_u x_{ui}
\qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad \Phi_i(L_i, x_{1i},\ldots,x_{Ni}; A_i) \geq y_i,
\label{eq:cost-min}
\end{equation}
where $A_i$ is productivity. With a unit cost $c_i$, constant returns imply a total cost
$c_i(p,w_i;A_i)\,y_i$, and perfect competition
equates price and unit cost, $p_i = c_i(p,w_i;A_i)$. Input demands follow
from Shephard's lemma, $L_i = y_i\,\partial c_i/\partial w_i$ and
$x_{ui} = y_i\,\partial c_i/\partial p_u$. 
We take $\Phi_i$ to be a nested CES (elasticity $\epsilon$ between labour and
the intermediate composite, $\theta$ among intermediates). We choose this functional form because it sits comfortably between two paradigmatic conceptions of production -- the inflexible, recipe-based Leontief production and the more adaptive Cobb-Douglas production function where substituiton is governed by marginal productivity. Formally, it can reproduce the respective outcomes at corners solutions (SOURCE).
% TODO können wir da smit den parameters angeben; betrifft dann theta und epsilon, oder?

On the consumption side, a representative household
maximizes utility given its consumption expenditure $E^h$:
\begin{equation}
\max_{c^h} \; U(c^h)
\qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N p_i c_i^h \leq E^h.
\label{eq:utility-max}
\end{equation}
We again represent $U$ by a CES function with elasticity $\sigma$.

The shares of the table are its weights, so at
baseline prices the solutions to~\eqref{eq:cost-min}
and~\eqref{eq:utility-max} reproduce the table exactly. Equilibrium
requires that output satisfies intermediate and final demand in every
sector, the row identity~\eqref{eq:io-row} read as a market-clearing
condition.

These conditions determine prices and quantities only once wages,
employment and household resources are pinned down. The table and the
optimization problems are silent on both: a closure supplies them.
\endgroup

\subsection{The welfare measure}\label{subsec:welfare}

The approach above gives into estimate for \emph{Real GDP} based on inspecting
the wage bill $w\sum_i L_i$. For scenarios with price changes the wage bill
is deflated to reflect changes in purchasing power\footnote{Specifcally, we use a seven-component T\"ornqvist price index of final demand (consumption, government purchases and the programme, investment, exports, and the three import and tax leaks booked in Appendix~\ref{app:kernel})}. Under a demand-only shock every price is pinned at its baseline value in the majority of the scenarios captured by table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5}, so the deflator is exactly one and real GDP moves
with the employment index $w\sum_i L_i$ alone. Specifically, in our setup deflation is only required for the no-reallocation and the elastic labor supply closures. 

However, as real GDP measures how much is produced, not how well the household fares, different assumptions on the valuation of this output can be made and, again, such assumptions oft represent foundational theoretical viewpoints. A conventional approach for our problem would be to employ a \emph{T\"ornqvist welfare index}, that compares household consumption bundle against its baseline. For the closed economy it coincides with real GDP; in an open economy, however, it does not. In practical terms this measure of welfare will be rather sensitive to the financial closure applied -- specifically, only the assumption that households repurpose spending for some reason (F1) will avoid a negative impact on consumption, while shfting expenditures in the public secto will automatically reduce welfare. In this spirit, negative welfare impacts in scenarios for other financial closures are more of an accounting artefact than a real economic effect as, for the purpose of a transformational stimulus, we would take no harm to assume that public and private spending would supply a quite similar and comparable bundle of goods. Against this backdrop that both types of spending could easily lead to the same outcome it is difficult to sustain the assumpton than public expenditures have no impact on welfare.

Alternatively, and because the representative household has homothetic CES preferences, the welfare assessemnt could directly honor this assumption by employing an /emph{exact} money-metric assessment of welfare change: with the expenditure function $e(p,u)=u\,\Pi(p)$, the change in real consumption expenditure $E^h/\Pi(p)$ is the consumption-equivalent (equivalent variation) of the shock. Under the consumption-price numeraire $\Pi(p)=1$ this measure is simply the change in after-tax, post-saving household resources $E^h$; so it coincides with the quantity index where prices do not move and corrects it in the price-moving sectoral and no-reallocation rows, where the index is only a second-order approximation. Hence, it seems to be the preferable, more precise baseline choice, although still only focused on private expenditure.

Further variations of these indices can be obtained when shifting the definition of what, exactly, is valued by the welfare index. One obvious take here is to introduce a final demand index that captures not only net household consumption but also the spending programme as such, which then undergoes further valuation. A second option is to include leisure as a standalone variabel that determines social welfare jointly with economic output. And, indeed, the options above are not only silent on public expenditure but also on the the disutility of the additional work, which
matters for all closures where employment absorbs the shock.

In what follow we will show three different indices for all closure scenarios: the conventional T\"ornqvist index and a CES-based evaluation taking into account final demand instead of household consumption. In addition, we include a version with a leisure term, where we assume that household perceive supplying additional labor as especially burdensome (setting $\eta_s=0.5$) for those scenarios where a leisure term is not alredy defined.
