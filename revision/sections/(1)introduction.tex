\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

%TODO: the motivation has changed so we need to do some research for new sources

Input-output models are an established tool for estimating the economic effects of structural policy choices related to public investment, industrial policy or labor market trends \citep{BlairMiller}. Against this background it does not come as a surprise that input-output models are also widely used to estimate the economic effects of additional investment undertaken in the course of a socio-ecological transformation aiming to render social provisioning processes carbon-neutral \citep{Leontief.1970, Hornykewycz.2025, Markandya.2016, Markaki.2013}. However, it is well known that the results obtained from such models depend crucially on the underlying axiomatic setup. Variations in outcomes emerging from variations of the chosen setup are seldom reported in the literature on transformation efforts that resorts to input-output modeling. Moreover, and more importantly, little attention is paid to the fact that the axiomatic setup of input-output models reflects economically important contextual arguments. In this paper we recapitulate that substantial variation in outcomes is due to a specific part of the axiomatic setup, namely the different closure choices. For research on socio-ecological transformation efforts it is important to recognize that such closure choices are not mere technical decisions, but carry substantial economic content; they capture -- or presuppose -- certain contextual arguments, assumptions about the current state of the economy, the treatment of the future and the exact form in which some policy proposal is executed in practice.

Closure choices carry substantial economic content as they make (implicit) assumptions about the context and conditions under which the suggested policy is implemented. This observation resonates with arguments on the importance of socio-historical and institutional context in economics \citep{Lawson1985, Dobusch.2012} as neatly summarized by Keynes's quip that "economics is a science of thinking in terms of models joined to the art of choosing models which are relevant to the contemporary world" \citep{Keynes.1938}. In this spirit our argument is also grounded in the observation that closure choices often reflect traditional divides in economic theory and policy so that, in effect, old ghosts haunt modern applications of input-output models, which have become a main workhorse in modeling socio-ecological transformation efforts \citep{Garrett.2017, Hornykewycz.2025, Markandya.2016, Markaki.2013}. 

It is well known that such paradigmatic cleavages matter greatly for input-output analysis: while the question whether or to what relative extent output or prices react to a change in final demand (scarcity vs. output-adjustment) is most prominent as it sits at the core of the division between computable general equilibrium models (CGEs) and the more traditional linear extrapolations of production structures based on the Leontief Inverse \citep{Leontief.1936, BlairMiller} Moreover, a series of other conceptual questions come into view when reflecting on paradigmatic cleavages in input-output modeling: choices on production technology (Cobb Douglas vs. Leontief; \citealp{Labini.1995, Klump.2012, Leontief.1936}), financing (external vs. internal; \citealp{BlinderSolow.1973, Hein.2023}), policy implementation (incentivising private actors vs. state-led action) or technological change (exogenous vs. endogenous; \citealp{Kaldor1961}) are all examples of such foundational questions, where disagreement translates into a structural wedge on received estimates and projections. In this paper we show that such conceptual questions have a substantial impact on the estimated effects of socio-ecological transformation efforts and which of these questions have the strongest impact on final results. To substantiate and illustrate this claim we use the case of a well-understood green transformation policy, the decarbonization of the German housing sector, and show how different axiomatic setups imprint on final estimates. By doing so, we hope to provide applied researchers with better guidance for reflecting on how the diversity of available interpretations of input-output models impacts on their problem at hand.

In what follows we will specifically focus on two types of closures, namely labor market closures and financing closures, which can be mapped onto a configuration space with two categorical axes.
In this space one axis goes from fundamental scarcity of factors (i.e. fully utilized factor markets) to flexible output adjustment (in the spirit of the Keynesian multiplier), which leads to fundamentally different conceptions on the short run. The second axis focuses on financing and goes from the assumption of immediate refinancing (additional expenditures lead to cuts elsewhere) to assuming some form of external financing that relieves the contemporary economy from immediate refinancing needs. The table is deliberately kept simple: it collapses the labour-market axis into two poles and is silent on magnitudes. The paper's central object is the finer grid behind it -- a five-by-three closure matrix (five labour-market closures crossed with the three financing regimes above), evaluated on a single, empirically informed green-investment impulse. Running that grid illustrates our main message, namely there is no closure-free multiplier. Every estimate of a transformation programme presupposes a short run and a financing regime, and that choice is not a technicality -- it is first-order for the magnitude, and occasionally for the sign, of the result. The matrix then identifies where closure choice is redundant and where it decides the answer. The two fixed-wage rows -- the pegged real wage and its Leontief fixed-coefficient corner -- coincide exactly, mimicking the basic intuition of Table~\ref{tab:overview}; the row with elastic sectoral labour supply, by contrast, is a distinct economy.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:io-bridge} connects transformation research to input--output modeling and introduces the green-investment impulse. Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} develops the two primitive questions -- the nature of the short run and the role of money -- and the simplified matrix. Section~\ref{sec:kernel} presents the common kernel, the five labor-market closures and the three financing regimes. Section~\ref{sec:application} runs the programme through the matrix, and Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only} considers shocks beyond demand-only impulses. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the results and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Proofs and calibration details are collected in the appendices.
