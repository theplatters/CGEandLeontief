\section{Application: the program through the matrix}\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Aggregate results}\label{subsec:aggregate}

The main results for applying the demand stimulus as described in section \ref{sec:io-bridge} to a set of model economies as conceptualized in sections \ref{sec:two-questions} and \ref{sec:kernel} are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} below. This table now asks a more direct question on the fifteen cells captured in our exposition: it does not focus on what happens on a level of formalities, but on what the happening is worth in terms of economic implications.

\input{tables/table4_results-5x3.tex}

We immediately observe that the overall output response to the demand shock for the core neoclassical models in the two leftmost columns is close to zero and that, as already indicated, out of these two only the no-reallocation is able to generate price reactions for the simple one-factor model under consideration. And indeed, the exact neutrality of the standard CGE column is a property of a demand-only shock under constant returns.

The response of GDP in the elastic labor supply also remains marginal relative to the size of the stimulus ($+0.12\%$ to $+0.19\%$ vs. $+1.33\%$ in stimulus size), but still paints a slightly more flexible picture of labor market conditions.\footnote{Because the elastic-supply responses are a monotone in $\eta_{s,i}$ --- output, employment and consumption rise with it while the price response falls --- we report that column as an interval rather than at a single elasticity. The full ladder of executed variants is in Appendix~\ref{app:variants}.} However, prices do move in this scenario, which, similar to the no-reallocation case, points to the capacity of sectorally differentiated models to produce price changes with conventional signs out of an otherwise minimal set of assumptions. 

In most of the flexible wage scenarios the economy provides external finance, where the genuinely minimal effect is amplified for the no-reallocation scenario due to higher prices for exports and investment. Moreover, for the latter two financing closures we actually observe the same reaction for these three models and two rows collapse into one. These collapsed results are accompanied by a large drop in estimated welfare $W_C$, which is derived from a direct application of the standard \emph{T\"ornqvist welfare index} as described in section \ref{sec:kernel}. This latter observation implies that the differences in $\Delta W_C$ across these scenarios are largely accounting mechanics: private expenditures in F1 are considered relevant for welfare, public expenditures not -- an assumption that will be relaxed further below.

Another simplifying collapse we observe in this assessment is that the employment-adjustment model and the Leontief closure do behave identically in the chosen setup as under a demand-only shock: with a pinned wage different substitution elasticities have no means to impact the outcome via relative prices. Results for financing closures reflect a Haavelmo pattern where spent taxes outweigh absorbed income in F2 relative to F1. Nonetheless, in these cases the projections do not differ markedly from the neoclassical core model with only slight changes in GDP predicted. This stands in contrast to the more voluminous expansion observed in the case of financing closure F3, where the full program (of $1.33\%$ of GDP) is financed externally. The stark differences in welfare estimates can be explained with recourse to the already mentioned private-public distinction (which drives much of the differences between F1 and F2) and the specific role of F3, where externally financed expenditures create additional income (equal to GDP growth), while higher GDP also implies a reduction of the net tax rate, which gives an additional boost on $W_C$ in this scenario.

In sum, we find that the matrix describes nine distinct economies rather than fifteen: the wage-pinned and Leontief columns coincide bit-for-bit, and under the three flexible-wage columns the tax-financed and the externally financed variants do so as well. The demand impulse is identical in every cell, so what separates the columns is never the size of the program but the margin through which the economy is allowed to absorb it --- and the two axes along which we have organized the closures, the labor market and the financing of the program, contribute unequally to the answer. The outcomes also underscore our main point, namely that closure choices on factor markets and financing conditions matter greatly for studying the economic impacts of transformation effort. GDP responses range from being slightly negative due to allocation frictions in the no-reallocation scenario to a multiplier driven expansion in the externally financed employment-adjustment cases.

In addition to these observations on the importance of closure choice we have also seen that welfare evaluations can show some differences to GDP estimates. While the latter are typically of greater interest in applied transformation research employing input-output modeling precisely because they avoid such an additional step of valuation, it is helpful to indicate the diverse paths by which our economic findings can be mapped unto social welfare. This is so because a scenario of an economic repurposing or stimulus in the spirit of a socio-ecological transformation invites us to explicitly consider questions of valuation that potentially underpin specific transformation efforts.

To this end table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison} below reweighs the economic outcomes shown in different scenarios according to a greater variety of welfare measures, starting from a reproduction of the T\"ornqvist $W_C$ in the first column. This measure is then complemented first by $W_D$ which explicitly adds the investment program content to the relevant consumption basket to signify the fact that private actors are the final recipients of said expenditures. Moving to this measure rectifies the gap to GDP change for the flexible wage models and aligns outcomes across financing closures. Given the nature of transformational policy proposals involving such repurposing or stimulus $W_D$ is arguably a more valid candidate for welfare assessment, at least when assuming that citizens share some pro-environmental preferences.

In addition to that table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison} shows several measures taking the explicit CES-specifications of the utility functions into account, thereby assuming that the substitution elasticity $\sigma=0.9$.\footnote{For $\sigma=1$ the CES aggregate reduces to the Cobb--Douglas case, in which expenditure shares are constant, and it then coincides with the T\"ornqvist index of the \emph{same} bundle. The difference that remains between $W_{CES}$ and $W_C$ in the table is therefore the inclusion of the program rather than the functional form; the equivalence also requires the preference weights to be unchanged, so it does not extend to the F1 rows.} The first one of these $W_{CES}$ rationalizes the new bundle of final demand -- that is, consumption plus the spending program as already in $W_D$ -- in terms of the preferences associated with the original bundle. Hence, the change here captures the welfare impact if we assume that the existing optimal allocation of consumer spending is disturbed by the stimulus (or the repurposing) and, hence, induces a welfare loss. This is also the reason why $W_{CES}$ gives comparatively skeptical evaluations of the program across the board.

The alternative specification shown, $W_{CES^*}$, in contrast assumes that the associated repurposing of spending is actually embraced by the population and that the new equilibrium is internalized. Such an assumption would come quite naturally for all scenarios with financing closure F1, which literally models a repurposing of spending by private actors. And while it might come less naturally for other scenarios the principal claim that some public effort is required to enable citizens to live up to their pro-environmental preference \citep{Wissen.2021,Braganza.2025} does not ride without evidence. In formal terms, we define $W_{CES^*}$ to compare the value of the new bundle (with $g$ subsumed into the private part) with updated preferences to the value of the old bundle at now dated preferences –- a procedure that leads to the most optimistic welfare projections of the variants presented in table \ref{tab:welfare-comparison}.

The final row of welfare estimate shown in Table~\ref{tab:welfare-comparison} adds the labor side to the valuation. Every measure up to this point values the bundle alone; the final row showing $W_L$ now also accounts for the additional work that a program requires -- so that a gain paid for in additional hours of labor no longer counts as a free gain. The leisure costs imposed by additional employment (or the benefits accruing from its reduction) are extrapolated from the paid wage whereas the remaining setup follows the one imposed for $W_{CES^*}$. Hence, we observe the same values for $W_L$ as we did for $W_{CES^*}$ in the first two scenarios, where employment remains effectively unchanged. For the remaining scenarios it lies mostly below $W_{CES^*}$ mimicking the expansion of employment typically observed thereby strongly discounting welfare gains especially for those scenarios that show the strongest expansion in employment. The one exception to these pattern are the two wage-pinned scenarios under financial closure F1, where employment slightly decreases, which is captured as a welfare benefit by $W_L$, but remains unseen by $W_{CES^*}$.

In sum, while a focus on GDP seems apt and adequate for many purposes in research aiming to connect input-output modeling and concerns for a socio-ecological transformation it has to be acknowledged that such concerns often have a behavioral dimension that is difficult to explicitly recognize in applied work. In this spirit our short exposition served to illustrate how specific welfare measures might deliver misleading results when it comes to assessing transformation policy efforts. Moreover, it also indicates which variations of measuring welfare could be helpful for researchers to make underlying assumptions on behavioral aspects explicit or to formulate potential scenarios for behavioral responses to a given policy.

\subsection{Sectoral results}\label{subsec:sectoral}

The aggregate figures in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} hide a strongly concentrated impulse. The program lands on only seven of the 71 sectors, and within these it is very unevenly distributed: it amounts to almost a quarter of baseline gross output in \emph{glass and glassware}, to about a tenth in \emph{specialized construction works} and in \emph{ceramic products, processed stone and clay}, to about five percent in \emph{rubber and plastics}, and to less than two percent in \emph{chemicals}, \emph{machinery} and \emph{electrical equipment}. Figure~\ref{fig:sectoral-comparison} compares three illustrative sectors across the no-reallocation, elastic labor supply and Leontief closures: a directly targeted sector (electrical equipment), an upstream supplier (metal ores and other mining), and a consumer service outside the program (other personal services). Within each labor closure, the bars span the three financing outcomes and the lines mark each financing cell. Figures~\ref{fig:sectoral-comparison-scatter} and \ref{fig:sectoral-comparison-scatter-f3} place all 71 sectors in the output--price plane for these three closures under the repurposing of consumption (F1) and external financing (F3), respectively. The detailed cell-by-cell panels, including all seven program sectors and the consumption-elasticity sensitivity, are in Appendix~\ref{app:sectoral-panels}.

A first observation is that the \emph{ranking} of the program sectors is the same in every cell: glass expands most, followed by ceramics and specialized construction, while machinery and electrical equipment barely move. What the closure decides is how much of the impulse is passed through to quantities and how much is absorbed by prices. Where labor is free to move at a given wage --- the standard CGE and the two fixed-wage columns --- prices stay at the baseline and the output response comes close to the impulse itself: glass expands by about 21\% on an impulse of about 23\%, and ceramics expands by more than its own impulse (about $+10.5\%$), because it also supplies the expanding construction sector with intermediates. Where the wage is sectoral, a part of the impulse is turned into prices instead. In the no-reallocation column the glass sector expands by only $+11.7\%$ (F1) to $+14.3\%$ (F2/F3), while its price rises by 22\% to 28\% and its wage by more than half; elastic labor supply lies in between, with glass output up by 17\% to 18\% and its price by about 10\%. The cross-closure scatters in Figures~\ref{fig:sectoral-comparison-scatter} and \ref{fig:sectoral-comparison-scatter-f3} show that this is a systematic pattern rather than a feature of a single sector: with sectoral wages, quantities and prices move together along upward-sloping sectoral supply curves, and the program sectors sit in the upper right corner, far away from the cloud formed by the other 64 sectors. Outside the program sectors, price changes remain small (within $\pm 1.7\%$ under elastic supply and $\pm 4.4\%$ under no-reallocation).

The second observation concerns the sectors that are \emph{not} targeted by the program. In eight of the nine economies the large majority of them contracts: between 51 and 56 of the 64 non-program sectors lose output, with a median change between $-0.3\%$ and $-0.6\%$. The losers are predominantly consumer-facing services --- \emph{travel agencies}, \emph{accommodation and food services} and \emph{other personal services} --- whose demand is squeezed by the reallocation of spending toward the program goods (F1) or by the taxes that finance it (F2). The few winners are upstream suppliers of the program sectors, most notably \emph{metal ores and other mining} ($+2.1\%$ to $+3.2\%$), \emph{wood products} and \emph{metal products}. In the no-reallocation column, \emph{building construction} and \emph{civil engineering} are among the strongest losers (about $-1\%$): with immobile labor they cannot share in the boom of their neighbour, specialized construction, but face its higher input prices. The exception is the externally financed Leontief economy (Figure~\ref{fig:sectoral-comparison-scatter-f3}; detailed panel in Figure~\ref{fig:sectoral-fixed-f3}): here all 64 non-program sectors expand, with a median of $+1.4\%$, because the program adds income instead of displacing other spending and employment is not capped. This is the sectoral face of the multiplier observed in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}, and it is the only cell in which the program is not, sector by sector, a reallocation from the rest of the economy to the seven program sectors.

Third, the consumption bars show how strongly the sectoral pattern of consumption depends on the financing closure. Under F1 the program is booked as repurposed private spending, so household final demand for the program goods rises dramatically (by 30\% to 130\% in five of the seven sectors). Under F2 and F3 the program is a separate demand component, and consumption of the program goods \emph{falls}. Where prices stay at the baseline, this fall is uniform across sectors and simply mirrors the change in aggregate consumption ($-1.8\%$ in the standard CGE column, $-1.5\%$ and $+2.3\%$ in the wage-pinned columns under F2 and F3), because with unchanged relative prices households scale their basket proportionally. With sectoral wages, by contrast, households substitute away from the goods whose prices rise: consumption of glass falls by 21\% under no-reallocation and by 10\% under elastic supply. It is also only here that the consumption elasticity matters visibly; across all other series and cells the appendix panels' error bars for $\sigma\in\{0.6,0.99\}$ are barely visible.

Finally, the figures underline the two collapses discussed above at the sectoral level. The standard CGE column and the wage-pinned columns produce almost the same sectoral quantity pattern under F1 and F2 (compare Figures~\ref{fig:sectoral-alpha-f1} and \ref{fig:sectoral-fixed-f1}); the small aggregate differences in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} stem from the level of employment, not from a different allocation across sectors. And the fixed-nominal-wage and Leontief closures coincide not only in the aggregate but sector by sector. In sum, which sectors gain and which lose is largely fixed by the input--output structure and the composition of the program, and it is robust across closures; how much the program sectors expand, whether this expansion shows up in quantities or in prices and wages, and whether the rest of the economy contracts or expands with it, is decided by the closure.
