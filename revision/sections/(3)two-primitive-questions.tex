\section{Two primitive questions, a simplified matrix and some complications}\label{sec:two-questions}

As already indicated, key points of disjuncture in input-output modeling can be mapped onto classic cleavages separating different traditions of economic thought. Such traditions can often be characterized in terms of the basic analytical ideas -- or ``rivers'' in the history of economic thought (SCREPANIT/ZAMGANI with p.) -- they endorse. Where these basic ideas have a different emphasis or contradict outright, a paradigmatic cleavage is in sight \citep{Dobusch.2012}. Two such classic fields of tension in economic thinking also lie at the heart of different approaches to input-output modeling: the nature of the short run and the role of money and credit, as already captured by Table~\ref{tab:overview}.

When mapping the rough distinctions from our introductory table more specifically to the realm of input-output models, we find that different labour market closures are the key entry point for different understandings of the short run: if production factors are fully utilized, increased monetary demand will shift wages and prices as capacity constraints bind. While this view is traditionally associated with computable general equilibrium models (CGEs), the core intution does not hold for models with just one production factor. Here, relative scarcity does not kick in so that prices and wages stay constant and demand is simply reallocated to other sectors –– the underlying trade-off materializes in a change in the composition of output but does not affect prices. However, as shown below with the example of the ``no reallocation'' model, prices do respond even in a one factor model when capacity constraints bind on a sectoral, instead of aggregate, level.

Conversely, if free factors of production are available, additional expenditures will lead to an expansion of employment and, correspondingly, output. This classic output adjustment closure \citep{Taylor1990} is what one gets from an application of the Leontief inverse. Both visions can be unified in one analytical apparatus (see \citealp{Robinson.2006} and Section~\ref{sec:kernel}), just differentiated by different assumptions on what is exogenous or endogenous when monetary expenditures increase. The two poles are the standard CGE closure, in which one flexible wage clears the labour market at full employment and additional demand is met by reallocation at given aggregate output, and the fixed real wage closure, in which the pegged wage pins prices so that employment and output absorb demand. Under demand-only shocks the latter reproduces the Type-II Leontief-inverse multiplier -- the inverse of the input-output matrix augmented by the induced round of household consumption -- exactly, and it does so at every value of the substitution elasticities; its fixed-coefficient corner, the Leontief closure, therefore yields the same result (Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}). While the former is the obvious neoclassical standard model, the latter reflects the intuition of a multiplier and a notion of economic planning that shows greater resonance with (Post-)Keynesian, radical and evolutionary approaches \citep{Pollin.2015,Mazzucato.2018}.

While the crosswise recombination of these different closure options already gives rise to some complexity, the underlying choice menu is nonetheless somewhat rough and -- in the case of the short-run -- simplistically dichotomous. Hence, it comes as no surprise that further established variations exist that try to address this point. An obvious starting point from a mainstream perspective to introduce greater variety in labor market outcomes is by explicitly referring to a leisure-income trade-off, which leads to the canonical \textit{elastic labor supply model}: Assuming that the substitution effect dominates, higher wages will attract additional labor so that capacity constraints do not bite with full force. In the \emph{elastic labor supply} closure the fixed labor endowment is replaced by a sector-specific labor supply curve, $L_i^{s} = \bar L_i\,[\,(w_i/\Pi(p))/(w_i^{0}/\Pi^{0})\,]^{\eta_{s,i}}$, with the elasticities set to one common value, $\eta_{s,i} \equiv \eta_s$. Two changes of functional form follow: labor supply becomes \emph{behavioural} rather than a quantity restriction or a price pin, and the single aggregate labor market becomes $N$ sectoral markets with $N$ wages.\footnote{The latter assumption is not strictly necessary: a single-wage form, with one aggregate supply curve $\sum_i L_i = \bar L\,[\,(w/\Pi(p))/(w^{0}/\Pi^{0})\,]^{\eta_s}$, is also possible. Under demand-only shocks, however, this single-wage form coincides with the standard CGE closure for every $\eta_s$ (Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}), so the sectoral form is what makes the elasticity matter in our case of application, which typically implies shocks with rather heterogeneous impact on sectors.}

While this modification brings greater responsiveness on the side of employment other variations in the mainstream literature assume forms of rigidity that go even beyond the conventional CGE labor market closure. A prominent recent example is given by \citet{BF2019}, who introduce a ``no-reallocation'' case as a baseline scenario to articulate the view that sectoral productivity shocks might impact on aggregate outcomes in a non-linear way. Formally, the transition from the standard CGE model to the no-reallocation model is governed by $\eta$, which represents the mobility of labor across sectors and admits two cases in our setup: $\eta=0$ for the no-reallocation case and $\eta=1$ for a full reallocation case that coincides with the standard CGE model.

Technically the \emph{Leontief closure} named here can be represented with the very same analytical apparatus as the conventional CGE model: with substitution elasticities taken to their Leontief limits, fully flexible production factors and the real wage pegged \eqref{eq:closure-delta}, prices then follow from constant costs and final demand determines output. This is the corner most applied transformation studies work with, usually without emphasis. Given this restrictive setup the \emph{Leontief closure} can itself be conceived as corner solution of a broader set of related approaches that retain the basic idea that ``quantities adjust'' without also committing to a Leontief production function -- although under demand-only shocks this corner is not distinct from the rest of the set: the fixed real wage closure yields the identical result at every value of the substitution elasticities, and the rigidity of production starts to matter only once supply shocks enter (Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}). Observing these additional models and corresponding closure variations moves our view on the short run from the Manichaean scenario formulated in table \ref{tab:closure-conditions} to a more nuanced exposition of different conceptions of the short run: the \textit{no-reallocation approach} imposes additional rigidity on labor markets, \textit{elastic labor supply models} allow for introducing behavioural responses and the \textit{fixed real wage models} try to generalize the scope of the output adjustment view. Moreover, the first two of these approaches are articulated on a sectoral level and the latter can be extended to one. In what follows we suggest to take the now resulting portfolio of closure choices as means to illustrate the consequences of different closure assumptions -- and, hence, paradigmatic perspectives -- on results when studying the impacts of a socio-ecological transformation by means of input-output models.

Before we map these five closures in a monumental table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5} further below, it makes sense to point out the sometimes surprising overlaps that arise for certain characteristic parameter values under demand-only shocks -- the case of our application -- as summarized by Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} below. For one, the no-reallocation model differs from the conventional CGE model only by exactly this ``no-reallocation'' assumption, whereas choosing a reallocation term $\eta=1$ instead of $\eta=0$ would lead to full convergence with the conventional case (as the sectoral level ceases to matter with ``full reallocation''). For another, the elastic labor supply model -- being a curve of solutions rather than a simple corner -- reproduces the no-reallocation model exactly when labor market participants lack any flexibility ($\eta_{s,i}=0$), and approaches the \textit{fixed real wage} model at the inverse position ($\eta_{s,i} \rightarrow \infty$). The latter is a limit rather than an attained case: the supply curves never become perfectly flat, and the elastic model keeps the net external transfer free, whereas the fixed real wage model pins it to zero; the elastic family therefore spans the two rigid closures without terminating in either. Hence, the elastic labor supply model can be said to sit at the centre of the interparadigmatic bridge between neoclassical interpretations based on factor scarcity and more Keynesian multiplier-style interpretations based on extrapolation via the Leontief inverse \citep{Robinson.2006, Taylor1990}.\footnote{Also, under demand-only shocks the elastic labor supply model collapses to the conventional CGE in its single-wage form, i.e.\ in the absence of sectoral wage heterogeneity, for every value of $\eta_s$.} Finally, as already indicated, under demand-only shocks the \textit{fixed real wage} closure is equivalent to the classic Leontief result whatever the substitution elasticities, so that the rigidity of production structures matters only once supply shocks enter. A more detailed exposition drawing on the basic framework as established in section \ref{sec:kernel} that rationalizes the overlaps between different closures is found in Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}.

Based on this core observation that a series of, partially overlapping, key axiomatic variations \citep{Kapeller.2013} exist when it comes to closure choice in input-output modeling we aim to map the basic framework as sketched in Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} to an applied level by asking: which of these assumptions will typically make what difference for estimating the economic effects of transformational policies. Moreover, as estimates for such economic effects often serve as a baseline for and precondition for estimating further ecological effects and also play a key role in informing political decision-making, the relevance of this question reaches far beyond the mere technical level. Following this agenda we combine the estimate of investment requirements introduced in section \ref{sec:io-bridge} across different model specifications to better reflect on how the conceptual distinctions introduced above matter for applied analysis and how different closure dimensions (here: labor markets and financing) interact in practice.
