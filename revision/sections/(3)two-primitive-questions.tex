\section{Two primitive questions, a simplified matrix and some complications}\label{sec:two-questions}

As already indicated, key points of disjuncture in input-output modeling can be mapped onto classic cleavages separating different traditions of economic thought. Such traditions can often be characterized in terms of the basic analytical ideas -- or ``rivers'' in the history of economic thought (SCREPANIT/ZAMGANI with p.) -- they endorse. Where these basic ideas have a different emphasis or contradict outright, a paradigmatic cleavage is in sight \citep{Dobusch.2012}. Two such classic fields of tension in economic thinking also lie at the heart of different approaches to input-output modeling: the nature of the short run and the role of money and credit, as already captured by Table~\ref{tab:overview}.

%##AG: shal we wed in some closure terms already in this paragraph to prepare tbale 2 – probably yes?
When mapping the rough distinctions from our introductory table more specifically to the realm of input-output models, we find that different labor market closures are the key entry point for different understandings of the short run: if labor is tight or fully utilized, increased monetary demand will increase wages and prices as capacity constraints bind as computable general equilibrium models (CGEs) typically suggest. Conversely, if free factors of production are available, additional expenditures will lead to an expansion of employment and, correspondingly, output. This classic output adjustment closure \citep{Taylor1990} is as what on gets from a simple linear extrapolation of the Leontief Inverse. Both visions can be unified in one analytical apparatus (see ROSBINSON UND PTHER POSER SOURCES? or \ref{sec:kernel}), just differentiated by different closures, i.e., definitions of what is exogenous or endogenous in the context of increasing monetary expenditures.

Financial closures on the other hand encode prior beliefs about the role of our monetary system and the nature of money. As input-output models are typically conceived as static, operating outside of time (see however AUSNAHMEN ZITIEREN??), there is no borrowing against the future -- additional expenditure cannot be externally financed in the conventional sense, but have to be repaid within the same period (see however AUSNAHMEN ZITIEREN??). Hence, while a traditional, simplified application of a Leontief inverse would suggest some external stimulus, exactly such an external force is at odds with the internal consistency of the model. In other words, such models implicitly build on an exogenous view on money and restrict the application of ideas on endogenous money creation as it lacks a corresponding model of the intertemporal role of debt.

%##AG: shal we wed in some closure terms already in this paragraph to prepare tbale 2 – probably yes?
Refinancing can then occur in three ways: by households -- the repurposing of consumption --, via the state -- due to increased taxation --, or by someone willing to take foreign debt to provide an external stimulus. The latter can thereby be used to roughly emulate an endogenous concept of money within an input-output framework without explicitly violating its internal consistency properties. Mapping these two dimensions onto specific modeling approaches allows for recasting Table~\ref{tab:overview} in terms of specific constellations of closure summarized by Table~\ref{tab:closure-conditions} below.

While the crosswise recombination of these different options on closure choices already gives rise to some complexity, the underlying choices are somewhat rough and -- in the case of the short-run -- simplistically dichotomic. Hence, it comes as no surprise that further established variation of closure setups exist that try to address this point. An obvious starting point from a mainstream perspective to introduce greater variety in labor market outcomes by explicitly refering to a leisure-income trade-off, which leads to the canonical \textit{elastic labor supply model}: Assuming that the substitution effect dominates, higher wages will attract additional labor so that capacity constraints do not bite with full force. In the \emph{elastic labour supply} closure the the fixed labour endowment is replaced by a sector-specific labor supply curve, $L_i^{s} = \bar L_i\,[\,(w_i/\Pi(p))/(w_i^{0}/\Pi^{0})\,]^{\eta_s}$, with one common elasticity $\eta_s$. Two changes of functional form follow: labour supply becomes \emph{behavioural} rather than a quantity restriction or a price pin, and the single aggregate labour market becomes $N$ sectoral markets with $N$ wages.\footnote{The latter assumption is not strictly necessary, but relevant for our case of application which typically implies shocks with rather heterogenous impact on sectors.}

%##AG: akin to our discussion of BETA we could wed in some closure terms in this paragraph?
While this modification can bring greater responsiveness on the side of employment other variations in the mainstream literature assume forms of rigidity that even go beyond the conventional CGE labor market closure. A prominent recent example is given by \citet{BF2019}, who introduce a ``no-reallocation'' case as a baseline scenario to articulate the view that sectoral productivity shocks might impact on aggregate outcomes in a non-linear way. In the context of our case of application -- a socio-ecological transformation that is tied to sector-specific shifts or injections in final demand -- such a baseline setup is especially pessimistic as it assumes labor market rigidity across sectors. As an effect, sectors with strong demand shocks are then hopelessly overwhelmed as workers from other sectors cannot ``reallocate'' to where capacity is needed. This result, however, resonates with intuition that additional rigidities on labor markets will complicate transformation efforts building on exactly such an reallocation of labor.\footnote{In this case assumptions deviate from the neoclassical baseline, but strengthen the general laissez-faire policy stance associated with its standard closure. Also note that related work on rigidities in capital or financial markets also exists, but is beyond the scope of this paper (see, however, (SOME SOURCES))}

As already implicit in table \ref{tab:closure-conditions} the classical Leontief price-quantity system, where final demand determines output, can represented by the very same system: just replace CES substitution by fixed technical coefficients and peg the real wage to arrive at an output adjustment interpretation. This is the corner most applied transformation studies work with, usually without saying so. Variation of this closure often keep the addumption of a fixed real wage, but nonetheless impose constraints on the availability of labor. Depdending on its specific formulation, it can emulate outcomes of conventional CGEs and output adjustment models, depending on how rigidity in this model setup is exactly specified. It is for these reasons, that the fixed real wage model is at the heart of the interparadigmatic bridge between neoclassical interpretations based on factor scarcity and more Keynesian multiplier-style interpretations focusing on extraolation via the Leontief Inversem that has been developed and in the last decades (REF: ROBINSON?
