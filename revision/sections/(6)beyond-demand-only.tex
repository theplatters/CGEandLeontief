\section{Complexifying demand-only shocks}\label{sec:beyond-demand-only}

\subsection{Kaldor--Verdoorn dynamics}\label{subsec:pricing-capacity}

A key observation from the preceding section is that the one-factor model shows a limited price response, and one that appears only where the labor market is sector-specific: the deflator moves in the no-reallocation and elastic-supply columns and stays at one in the other nine cells of Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}. The nature and scope of a transformational stimulus --- especially in a long-term setting --- nevertheless invite reflection on how a permanent shift in demand could affect production in the short and medium run: will additional demand enable firms to exploit greater economies of scale? can it spur research and investment, so that production becomes more effective over time? or will it induce capacity pressure, thereby reducing efficiency? Such concerns -- which can be reformulated as the question of where we sit on the standard cost curve -- would indeed impact prices and they can be captured on a different level, namely that of technology.

The most straightforward way to express this intuition formally is to let
effective technology respond to utilization,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capacity-door}
  A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i \;=\; A_i\,\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i}\Bigr)^{-\delta_i\,\alpha_i},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_i$ as the sector's baseline gross output and $\alpha_i$ the factor (value-added) share, so that $\delta = 0$ reproduces the model of the preceding sections exactly.
The elasticity $\delta_i$ is a reduced-form utilization elasticity of \emph{value-added} productivity: no factor is added and no income block is created, because the productivity change passes into unit cost under zero profit, weighted by the factor share. A Hicks-neutral gain $d\ln A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i$ then raises value added per worker by $d\ln A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i/\alpha_i$ (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-hooks}), so $-\delta_i$ is directly comparable with an estimated Verdoorn coefficient.\footnote{Applied to gross output without the factor share, the same bracket would overstate the value-added response by $1/\alpha_i$: factor shares below one half turn $\delta = 0.5$ into an elasticity above one, and into $11.6$ for coke and refined petroleum ($v \approx 0.04$).}

The two signs of $\delta$ again carry two traditions, underpinned by two distinct visions of the typical operating mode of an economy. With $\delta > 0$ scarcity gains an additional bite: unit costs rise with own output --- capacity pressure, tight supply chains, higher risk and, hence, eventually higher costs and prices (even if, as here, market power considerations are bracketed out). With $\delta < 0$, on the other hand, unit cost falls with output --- the dynamic increasing returns articulated early by \textcite{Verdoorn1949} (see also \citealp{Kaldor1966} and \citealp{SylosLabini1995}).\footnote{With $\delta_i < 0$ a sector's unit cost falls as its output rises --- a positive feedback that can admit several equilibria. Every run with $\delta \neq 0$ is therefore reached by continuation in $\delta$ from the $\delta = 0$ case and re-solved from four starting points; a result is accepted only if the converged starts agree to $10^{-6}$ and the residual Jacobian has full rank. Every result with $\delta \neq 0$ in the executed generation passes this check (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-guard} reports the design and the measured spreads).}

We run scenarios with $\delta = \pm 0.5$ for manufacturing to illustrate the impact of the Verdoorn coefficients on estimated outcomes. These effects are confined to the
goods sectors (CPA sections A--F: agriculture, mining, manufacturing, utilities
and construction), following \textcite{Kaldor1966}, who reads Verdoorn's law
as a law of manufacturing, not of services. Moreover, this setup matches the incidence of the program, whose direct demand falls entirely on goods sectors. The counterfactual that extends the elasticity to the services sectors as well is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:variants}.\footnote{Extending the elasticity to the services sectors also leaves the sign of the price response unchanged in the four closures where quantities absorb the shock --- the deflator still rises with $\delta > 0$ and falls with $\delta < 0$. However, the sign reverses where the real wage is pinned: at $\delta = -0.5$ the deflator then rises by 0.14 and 0.03 percent under preference reallocation and tax finance and falls only under external finance ($-0.82$ percent). The main reason for this is that service sectors  lose demand in this scenario, which translates into lower productivity -- and that loss is passed straight into prices.}

Table~\ref{tab:pricing-kaldor} reports the results for such scenarios by building on the outcomes already shown in Section~\ref{sec:application} to cover the full matrix of approaches discussed for scenarios with endogenously increasing ($\delta = -0.5$) and decreasing ($\delta = +0.5$) productivity effects. For these values we observe small changes in the aggregate price level across all scenarios relative to the baseline applications. Deviations from baseline values thereby follow a similar pattern: all outcomes show the same sign as well as similar magnitudes imposing net changes of the price level ranging from $0.05\%$ to about $0.4\%$ with the large majority of estimates lying below $0.2\%$. While all models now show some response in prices, the no-reallocation and the elastic labor supply model -- which also came with price responses already in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} --- still show the most pronounced change in prices on both, aggregate as well as sectoral levels (the latter is partially captured by $\max |\Delta p|$).

These price changes reflect efficiency gains and losses due to the additional demand that have different implications for production and employment across different model specifications. For labor market closures where scarcity governs economic reactions -- that is, the first three closure specifications in our matrix -- increasing technological capabilities ease these scarcity constraints so that production can expand. For the output adjustment closures with a pinned real wage on the other hand,  increasing efficiency leads to lower employment effects and, correspondingly, (slightly) lower impacts on net welfare because the size of output adjustment is determined ex ante (and can now be achieved with fewer inputs).

Notably, we observe that the different financial closures have close to no impact on how the economy responds to introduction of Kaldor--Verdoorn effects for the models based on constrained factor supply, whereas it has, as in the preceding section, a stronger influence on the outcome of the quantity adjustment model based on fixed wages. In the no-reallocation, standard-CGE and elastic-supply closures, tax finance and external finance continue to produce identical real outcomes when productivity responds to output. Moreover, we observe that the perfect correspondence between the fixed-wage and Leontief columns observed for the baseline case breaks for any $\delta \neq 0$: in such a case, where prices move, substitution elasticities matter again and the coincidence dissolves. A similar discrepancy is observed for the positive Kaldor--Verdoorn term. In general, the fixed wage model thereby shows greater responsiveness in output to technological change as compared to the Leontief closure: Under external finance a negative Kaldor--Verdoorn term implies an increase of employment by $1.35\%$ against the $1.48\%$ shown for the Leontief closure.

\subsection{Markup-Pricing and distributional assumptions}\label{subsec:pricing-markup}

While Kaldor-Verdoorn dynamics capture the idea that firms become successively more efficient when supplying greater quantities, such an increase in firm size can also lead to a corresponding increase in market power. Similarly, effective markups are said to rise with utilization, which points to the relevance of distributional considerations associated with final demand shocks in input-output models featuring multiple production factors. These considerations resonate with the observation that pricing power that moves with demand has a long pedigree in economics \citep{Kalecki1954,Bils1987}. Taking the latter formulation -- markups rising with capacity utilization -- as a vantage point and projecting it onto a sectoral level in line with the basic setup from Section~\ref{sec:kernel}, we arrive at the following formulation for prices 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:markup-door}
  p_i \;=\; \mu_i\,c_i, \qquad \mu_i \;=\; 1 + \kappa\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i} - 1\Bigr),
\end{equation}
which is nested at $\kappa = 0$ so that the core framework from section \ref{sec:kernel} remains unchanged. The parameter has a direct reading reflecting our theoretical rationale: at $\kappa = 0.5$ a ten percent expansion lifts the margin to $\mu_i = 1.05$, and the largest margin realised across the runs below is $1.11$, at a sectoral output response of about $22$ percent. What does change is the income side: Section~\ref{sec:kernel} reads value added off the wage bill, because labor is its only primary factor, whereas with a markup value added is the wage bill plus the profit bill defined below (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-accounting}). Introducing such a pricing equation gives profits an explicit role, which is why this section departs from the hitherto used benchmark case of zero profits and introduces profits as a second income foundation, without explicitly modeling capital as a second factor of production. Hence, this feature is introduced in a minimal way: firms still hire labor and inputs at \emph{marginal cost} $c_i = p_i/\mu_i$, while households and other firms buy at $p_i$; the
four places where the price enters a first-order condition are re-derived
accordingly in Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-door}. The only difference is now that profits enter as a pure surplus (akin to the classic definition of a rent), which eventually must go somewhere, which is why an additional assumption is required for profits as a novel stream of income.
Written out, that surplus is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profit-bill}
  \Pi \;=\; \sum_i \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{\mu_i}\Bigr) p_i y_i ,
\end{equation}
the sum over sectors of revenue minus the marginal-cost bill, zero whenever
$\kappa = 0$ and positive exactly where prices exceed marginal cost.

Regarding the question how profit income is used we rely on a simple formulation that captures the share of profit income that is retained, $s_\pi$, which allows for representing different scenarios. Taking $s_\pi = 1$, for instance, would represent cases where profits are fully retained by firms or foreign asset holders and do not feed back into the economy. In addition to such a scenario of ``full retention'', which is shown for $\kappa=0.25$ and $\kappa=0.5$, we also derive two additional scenarios where profits are treated as normal income. For these scenarios we show two different runs that set the savings rate on profits $s_\pi$ to $0.5$ and to the overall savings rate $s \approx 0.12$, respectively.\footnote{The augmented household budget is $E = (1-\tau)w\sum_i L_i + F + (1-s_\pi)\Pi$: the distributed share of the surplus enters beside after-tax labor income and the external transfer, and the household saves the share $s$ of the total as before, so $s_\pi = 1$ returns the kernel's budget exactly. Section~\ref{sec:kernel} leaves $E^h$ open because a closure pins it down; the retained part $s_\pi\Pi$ is booked as a leak in the external account (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-accounting}).}

A first observation associated with these results is that markup pricing unsurprisingly increases prices under all scenarios observed. The small average change across scenarios shown in the first column thereby has to be contrasted with the maximum price change across sectors shown in the second column, where strong price changes are mostly associated with the sector affected by the demand shock (ADD REF TO FIG?). At $\kappa = 0.5$ the markup's largest sectoral deviation lies between $9.5\%$ and $35\%$ for the flexible wage models and between $9\%$ and $14\%$ for the models with a pinned real wage. However, as the former already show price changes in some baseline scenarios, the relative impact due to the introduction of markup pricing is larger for the latter group. Hence, while a comparison with Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} indicates that the relative impact of this change on the flexible wage model is more limited, it still accounts for up to 10 percentage points for the conventional CGE model. In the no-reallocation panel the comparison is starkest: the deflator rises by $0.86$ percent while the profit bill amounts to $0.06$ percent of value added, so almost the whole price movement there reflects the dispersion of sectoral wages rather than the markup. In the no-reallocation and elastic supply panels, therefore, the larger share of the price movement is driven by wages rather than by the markup pricing imposed here.

The second obvious structural feature of the so obtained results is that the outcomes for the three flexible wage models depend only on the intensity of markups ($\kappa$), but not on how these markups are used. This is because the net external transfer $F$ adjusts flexibly and falls when the share of distributed profits increases, and vice versa. The same mechanism also produces the well-known equivalence between F2 and F3 observed for these models. In contrast, distributing profits raises $W_D$ and employment relative to retention in every financing column of the fixed-wage and Leontief panels, most so under external finance. Distribution is also inflationary where the wage is pinned: at $\kappa = 0.5$ routing the profit to households rather than retaining it raises the deflator from $1.01$ to $1.83$ percent under the fixed-wage closure and from $1.03$ to $1.92$ percent under the Leontief closure. The reason for both effects is simply that if the extra profit is spent rather than hoarded, the effective multipliers associated with the original stimulus become correspondingly larger. As with the Kaldor-Verdoorn dynamics, the coincidence between the fixed-wage and Leontief columns dissolves once prices move: in this context, the les flexible Leontief closures shows slighly higher price changes and, correspondingly, smaller growth of employment and output.

The welfare measure is the same as in Table~\ref{tab:pricing-kaldor}: $\Delta W_D$, the
quantity index of demand over $c+g$, now evaluated for a household that also
receives the distributed profit. Two caveats bound the reading: the markup is
symmetric about the baseline, so a contracting sector prices at a discount
($\mu_i < 1$; the markups recorded across these runs span $0.9918$ to $1.1107$),
and leaving profits untaxed understates the fiscal response under tax finance. Retention also costs welfare where the transfer is external: in the $F3$ column $W_D$ falls from $3.20$ without the door to $1.78$ under the fixed wage and $1.55$ under Leontief at $\kappa = 0.5$, and rises to $3.40$ and $3.10$ once the surplus is distributed.
