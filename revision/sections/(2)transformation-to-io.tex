\section{From transformation research to input--output models}\label{sec:io-bridge}

Modest proposals for decarbonization initiatives and large-scale suggestions for fundamental transformation both imply a repurposing of spending, either by private agents, the public sector or an interaction of both. Hence, such programmes share a similarity with traditional public spending programs (e.g SOURCE, ZB POLLIN), which also invite reflection on the economic impacts of such repurposed or additional spending. Classic concepts that come to mind in this context are the Keynesian cross (implicitly building on the idea of external financing), the Haavelmo theorem (which just states that the state -- in contrast to households -- spends all of its income) or the idea of a ``crowding out'' of public spending (similar to Haavelmo, but typically with adverse effects due to less investment) %TODO CLASSIC SOURCES RAUSSUCHEN). 

Several contributions in the recent literature (SOURCE; MAZZUCATO AND THE LIKE) exploit this similarity in various ways arguing that public spending is a functional means or necessary requirement for decarbonization and socio-ecological transformation. Aside from such more technocratic takes, a series of authors identify political synergies on this basis, where public spending alleviates both, environmental degradation as well as social exclusion, and, hence, serves as a foundational keystone for proposals on a just transition. Moreover, analytical approaches to input-output models sometimes emphasize their capability to identify structural dependencies and critical junctures, allowing for assessing the resilience and versatility of economies in times of intense transitions (SOURCE; DOYNE FARMER BOOK).

Finally, as a traditional application of input-output models in the spirit of a simple application of a Leontief inverse assumes an output expansion analogous to the Keynesian cross, this basic intuition of synergy can also be found on a more analytical level -- albeit only when taking, maybe unconsciously, a specific paradigmatic perspective. Given their analytical and conceptual advantages, their wide applicability and their resonance with contemporary political challenges it does not come as a surprise that also many applied studies in transformational research make use of input-output models to assess the potential impacts of such policies (LONG LIST OF SOURCES).

Our exemplary application goes to the heart of these debates by focusing on the key issue in transformation debates, namely the decarbonization of the residential sector. By taking Germany as an example we focus on a major European economy that is marked by a strong path dependence towards the use of fossil energy sources, especially in the residential sector, but also in energy production, which is key for providing alternative means of provisioning basic housing services. We start from a concrete estimate for additional investment requirements in Germany to arrive at a carbon-neutral residential sector by 2050 \citep{Hornykewycz.2025}. Such an effort would require more than doubling spending on renovation and increase annual expenditures on this by about 58 bn on average. By taking this number -- and the estimated sectoral distribution of spending needs -- as our vantage point, we anchor our illustration in an empirically and politically relevant case that is a valid example for the kind of ``repurposing of existing expenditures'' that a socio-ecological transformation would entail.

When doing so we base our analysis and discussion on a specific estimate of the costs associated with a transformation of the German housing sector towards carbon neutrality as supplied by \citet{Hornykewycz.2025}. This study estimates expected costs by proportionally scaling past renovations costs under the assumption that an increase in the renovation intensity takes place, that is sufficient to meet Germany's climate goals. Specifically, the study at hand finds that renovation efforts will have to be more than doubled, if Germany is set to comply with its overall goal to achieve carbon neutrality till 2050. Such a shift would amount to yearly costs of about 40.3 bn. € in 2019 prices\footnote{We assumed a deflator of 1.46 based on price indices for construction costs as specified in \citet{Hornykewycz.2025}. As the study also details how these costs should be mapped on different sectors, it provides sufficient information to construct a concrete and directly applicable specification for the sector-specific annual changes in final demand imposed by such a reorientation.
