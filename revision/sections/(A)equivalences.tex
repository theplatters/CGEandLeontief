\section{Equivalences, overlaps and scope of the closure matrix}\label{app:equivalences}

\noindent
The matrix of closures presented in Section~\ref{sec:application} crosses five
labor-market closures with three financing closures, and several of its
cells turn out to describe one and the same economy (effectively leading to nine numerically different scenarios). This appendix states the
conditions under which that happens, lists the coincidences with the
mechanism behind each, and closes with the algebra that produces them. The
exact derivations are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}\footnote{Model changes that
break the coincidences and restore variety across the matrix can be found in
Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}; the considerations from this appendix,
hence, do not necessarily apply there.}. In addition, this appendix clarifies
which overlaps between closure scenarios exist that are not directly visible
in, e.g., Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}.

\subsection*{Scope: three conditions}

\paragraph{A demand-only shock.} The program enters final demand at
baseline technology, without directly changing technology or the pricing equations. This assumption underpins the main case of our application.

\paragraph{Prices cannot see demand.} The zero-profit conditions
contain no demand variable and are homogeneous of degree one in prices and the
\emph{single} money wage, so relative prices are pinned by technology alone
(Lemma~\ref{lem:price}). A sectoral wage vector breaks exactly this: with $N$
wages the block reads $p=\pi(A,w)$ and demand reaches prices through the wage
structure. Under the consumption price index $\mathcal{P}(p)$ as numeraire the real wage then sits at its baseline anchor for every demand vector, every financing closure and every shock size (Corollary~\ref{cor:realwage}). 
% (sentence cut 2026-10-22 on the author's decision.)

\paragraph{A quantity block that is elasticity-free.} Evaluated at the baseline
prices $p=w=1$, the clearing system is exactly affine and independent of the
substitution elasticities $(\theta,\epsilon,\sigma)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:affine})
--- the production side of the classical nonsubstitution result
(Remark~\ref{rem:samuelson}). Every closure that pins the wage and lets
quantities absorb the shock therefore solves one and the same linear system, for
every substitution-elasticity triple at baseline technology and under a fixed
financing rule.

The second and third conditions also locate the boundary of the exercise,
since Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} introduces the single-wage branch and
sectoral wages as the matrix's two key approaches to labor-market dynamics:
where the wage is common the conditions apply as stated, where it is not they
lapse, and that is where the matrix regains variety.

The two sections below summarize coincidences across specifications: first
those sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:flexibility-map}, then those that the
results matrix prints.

\subsection*{Key coincidences in the background}

\paragraph{The four links.} Each of the three identities in
Table~\ref{tab:equiv-links} has a one-step argument, and the arguments are of
three different kinds; the fourth link is a limit that is not attained.

\emph{The switch.} There is nothing to prove for the first line: $\eta$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$, and its mobile value \emph{is} the cost-minimizing allocation, which is what the conventional closure assumes. The row earns its place by saying what the no-reallocation narrative is --- one switch away from the standard model, not a rival theory of the short run.

\emph{The single-wage supply.} The two systems differ in one equation only. At
the anchored real wage $w/\mathcal{P} = 1$ the supply equation reduces to
$\sum_i L_i = \Lbar$, which is the full-employment condition; the reduction is
an identity at every $\eta_s$ and under every financing rule
(Corollary~\ref{cor:realwage}).

\emph{The rigid corner.} Write the sectoral system and the $\eta=0$ endpoint in
the same four row blocks --- prices, quantities, wages, numeraire. At
$\eta_s=0$ sectoral labor supply becomes $\bar L_i$, so each sector's labor
equation reduces to $L^{cm}_i(p,y_i,w_i)=\bar L_i$; the endpoint's quantity block
evaluates the wage bill at exactly that frozen allocation. Each system's
solution therefore satisfies the other's equations, so their solution sets
coincide (Proposition~\ref{prop:nest}).

\emph{The link that is not an identity.} The arrow from the elastic labor supply model to
the employment-adjustment closure in Figure~\ref{fig:flexibility-map} is a
limit: as $\eta_s\to\infty$ the supply curves flatten, prices return to the baseline and the real wage to its anchor, and the elastic labor supply model converges to that
allocation at a non-zero external transfer. The two closures therefore agree in prices and in the wage anchor and differ in the transfer, which the elastic labor supply model keeps free and the employment adjustment model sets to zero; the limit exists, but it lies beside the closure rather than on it, and the
difference is the transfer. Section~\ref{app:sectoral} derives the limit, and
Table~\ref{tab:sectoral} measures it beside the closure at both transfers.

Of the three identities, the definitional one holds under any shock because
nothing in it refers to technology, the rigid-corner one because its argument
never uses $A\equiv\one$, and only the single-wage identity fails:
a technology change generally breaks the real-wage anchor
(Corollary~\ref{cor:realwage}).

\subsection*{Key coincidences in the closure matrix}

The two rows of Table~\ref{tab:equiv-matrix} rest on the two blocks below --- the
wage-pinned/Leontief pair is the quantity block read at a pinned wage
(Lemma~\ref{lem:affine}), the financing pair is the transfer instrument
\eqref{eq:finstrument}.

\paragraph{The price block.} Unit cost depends on prices and the wage alone,
\begin{equation}
c_i(p,w;A) = \Big( A_i^{\,\epsilon-1}\big( \alpha_i\, w^{1-\epsilon}
+ (1-\alpha_i)\, P_i(p)^{1-\epsilon} \big) \Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}},
\qquad
P_i(p) = \Big( \textstyle\sum_u \Omega_{iu}\, p_u^{\,1-\theta} \Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}},
\label{eq:appA-cost}
\end{equation}
and zero profit imposes $p_i = c_i(p,w;A)$ sector by sector. No demand
variable appears in that system, and it is homogeneous of degree one in
$(p,w)$: for any technology, every solution has the form $p = w\,\pi(A)$, and
\emph{relative} prices are functions of technology alone. With the
consumption price index $\mathcal{P}(p)$ as numeraire the real wage is therefore a
function of technology alone as well; at baseline technology it sits exactly
at its anchor $w/\mathcal{P}(p) = 1$. This is the second scope condition read as
algebra.

\paragraph{The quantity block.} Evaluated at those prices, goods-market
clearing is exactly affine in gross output,
\begin{equation}
y = G\,y + b(F), \qquad
G = \Omega^{\!\top}\mathrm{diag}(a)
  + (1-s)\,\mathrm{diag}(1-m)\,\tilde\beta\,\alpha^{\!\top},
\label{eq:appA-affine}
\end{equation}
and none of the substitution elasticities $(\theta,\epsilon,\sigma)$ enters
$G$ or $b(F)$, where $F$ is the net external transfer to households --- the
variable that clears the external account, and the one the flexible wage closures
solve for. The technique, and with it relative prices, is independent of the
composition of demand, which allocates quantities only. This is the third
scope condition read as algebra, and it is what makes the matrix a partition
of a few scalar regimes rather than fifteen separate models.

What the appendix does not establish is that the matrix is rigid in general.
It is rigid on the single-wage branch at baseline technology, and two different
things restore variety. Channels that let demand into the price block ---
sectoral labor markets, demand-sensitive markups, capacity constraints,
endogenous import prices --- restore identification. A supply shock does
something else: it moves technology, and with it the price block, breaking the
equivalences without demand entering them.
Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only} takes up both.
