\subsection{The pricing door I: capacity and Kaldor--Verdoorn}\label{subsec:pricing-capacity}
% Provenance: ADR-0029, ADR-0031, ADR-0032; runs pricing_capacity-v2-GAMMA-*,

A key observation from the preceding section is that the one-factor model shows a limited price response, and one that appears only where the labour market is sector-specific: the deflator moves in the no-reallocation and elastic-supply columns and stays at one in the other nine cells of Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}. The nature and scope of a transformational stimulus --- especially in a long-term setting --- nevertheless invite reflection on how a permanent shift in demand could affect production in the short and medium run: will additional demand enable firms to exploit greater economies of scale? can it spur research and investment, so that production becomes more effective over time? or will it induce capacity pressure, thereby reducing efficiency? Such concerns -- which can be reformulated as the question where we sit on the standard cost curve -- would indeed impact prices and they can be captured on a different level, namely that of technology.

The most straightforward way to express this intuition formally is to let
effective technology respond to utilisation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capacity-door}
  A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i \;=\; A_i\,\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i}\Bigr)^{-\delta_i\,\alpha_i},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_i$ as the sector's baseline gross output and $\alpha_i$ the factor (value-added) share, so that $\delta = 0$ reproduces the model of the preceding sections exactly.
The elasticity $\delta_i$ is a reduced-form utilisation elasticity of \emph{value-added} productivity, not a rental rate: no factor is added and no income block is created, because the productivity change passes into unit cost under zero profit, weighted by the factor share. A Hicks-neutral gain $d\ln A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i$ then raises value added per worker by $d\ln A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i/\alpha_i$ (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-hooks}), so $-\delta_i$ is directly comparable with an estimated Verdoorn coefficient.\footnote{Applied to gross output without the factor share, the same bracket would overstate the value-added response by $1/\alpha_i$: factor shares below one half turn $\delta = 0.5$ into an elasticity above one, and into $11.6$ for coke and refined petroleum ($v \approx 0.04$).}

The two signs of $\delta$ again carry two traditions, underpinned by two distinct visions of the typical operating mode of an economy. With $\delta > 0$ scarcity gains an additional bite: unit costs rise with own output --- capacity pressure, tight supply chains, higher risk and, hence, eventually higher costs and prices (even if, as here, market power considerations are bracketed out). With $\delta < 0$, on the other hand, unit cost falls with output --- the dynamic increasing returns articulated early by \textcite{Verdoorn1949} (see also \citealp{Kaldor1966} and \citealp{SylosLabini1995}). 
The door is solved inside the equilibrium residual rather than adjusted around it: $\delta$ enters the kernel's cost line, so every priced cell is an equilibrium in which the door is active (Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-door}).%

We run $\delta = \pm 0.5$, the order of the Verdoorn coefficients estimated
for manufacturing, as a bracket rather than a calibration, confined to the
goods sectors (CPA sections A--F: agriculture, mining, manufacturing, utilities
and construction). This follows \textcite{Kaldor1966}, who reads Verdoorn's law
as a law of manufacturing, not of services, and it matches the incidence of the
programme, whose direct demand falls entirely on goods sectors
(Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}). The counterfactual that extends the elasticity
to the services sectors as well is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:variants}.

Table~\ref{tab:pricing-capacity} reports the door under the programme of
Section~\ref{sec:application} on every labour closure; we read the fixed-wage
panel first. Its first result is
that the door works: with $\delta = \pm 0.5$ the largest sectoral price
deviation lies between 4.0 and 4.7 percent in every financing column, where the
fixed-wage column of Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} reports none. Its second
result is that Kaldor--Verdoorn for manufacturing lowers the GDP deflator under
every financing closure, by 0.19 to 0.35 percent
(\texttt{pricing\_capacity-v2-GAMMA-F1-dm05-s0}, \texttt{-F2-dm05-s0},
\texttt{-F3-dm05-s0}), while capacity pressure raises it. The sign is
nevertheless not a property of the door alone: extending the elasticity to the
services sectors, which receive none of the programme directly, reverses the
result under preference reallocation and tax finance ($+0.14$, $+0.03$,
$-0.82$ percent against $-0.19$, $-0.21$, $-0.35$;
\texttt{pricing\_capacity-v2-GAMMA-\{F1,F2,F3\}-dm05}), so that the sign
depends on which sectors carry increasing returns rather than on the financing
closure alone (Appendix~\ref{app:variants}). Under the CPI numeraire the same
door moves relative prices and the real wage instead: in the standard-CGE panel
Kaldor--Verdoorn lowers the deflator by 0.06 percent with employment fixed
(\texttt{pricing\_mobile-v2-ALPHA-\{F1,F2,F3\}-dm05-s0}), and in all three
CPI-gauge panels F2 and F3 remain indistinguishable: financing neutrality in
the mobile gauge survives the door.

Two consequences follow, and Table~\ref{tab:pricing-capacity} measures both.
The fixed-wage and Leontief columns coincide in Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix}
because both hold prices at the baseline, and their $\delta = 0$ rows coincide
here too. Once the door moves prices, substitution elasticities matter again and
the coincidence dissolves. Under external finance Kaldor--Verdoorn lowers the
deflator by 0.35 percent at the fixed wage and by 0.30 percent in the Leontief
closure, and raises employment by 1.35 and 1.48 percent respectively; the runs
are \texttt{pricing\_capacity-v2-GAMMA-F3-dm05-s0} and
\texttt{pricing\_capacity\_rows-v1-DELTA-F3-dm05-s0}. Likewise, elastic labour
supply differs from the standard CGE closure through the real wage, which the
door moves: Kaldor--Verdoorn raises employment in the elastic-supply panel from
0.12 to 0.16 percent under preference reallocation and capacity pressure lowers
it to 0.09 (\texttt{pricing\_capacity\_rows-v1-BETA-F1-\{d0,dm05-s0,dp05-s0\}}),
while the standard CGE closure holds employment fixed by construction. The
equivalences of
Section~\ref{subsec:equivalences} therefore hold on the slice ``$A \equiv 1$
\emph{and} price-invariant technology'', and every claim built on them carries
that clause.

\subsection{The pricing door II: the distribution markup}\label{subsec:pricing-markup}
% Provenance: ADR-0029 (Stage A), ADR-0030 (Stage B), ADR-0032; runs
% pricing_markup-v2-* and pricing_profit-v2-* (all five labour rows,
% OpenCGE v0.2.1; supersede the v1 markup runs); Table~\ref{tab:pricing-markup}.
The capacity door creates no rent. The second door does: a markup that rises
with utilisation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:markup-door}
  p_i \;=\; \mu_i\,c_i, \qquad \mu_i \;=\; 1 + \kappa\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i} - 1\Bigr),
\end{equation}
nested at $\kappa = 0$. Pricing power that moves with demand has a long
pedigree, from the degree of monopoly of \textcite{Kalecki1954} to the
procyclical marginal cost of \textcite{Bils1987}, and once the rent is paid
out it opens the wage-led versus profit-led question of
\textcite{BhaduriMarglin1990}. This section uses it only as a pricing
closure, not as a theory of distribution. Two modelling choices make
it a closure. First, firms still hire labour and inputs at \emph{marginal
cost} $c_i = p_i/\mu_i$, while households and other firms buy at $p_i$; the
four places where the price enters a first-order condition are re-derived
accordingly in Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-door}. Second, the rent
$\Xi = \sum_i (1 - 1/\mu_i)\,p_i y_i$ must go somewhere.

Table~\ref{tab:routing} lists the four places it can go. Foreign owners would
book it in the external account; a government wedge would make the state a
third financing instrument beside F2 and F3. We take \emph{retention} as the
baseline (the retained rows of Table~\ref{tab:pricing-markup}): the rent is booked as
saving by firms, the household budget is unchanged and all real effects run
through relative prices. Retention is the cheapest closure that keeps the
external-account identity exact; its cost, stated rather than implied, is that
retained funds are hoarded, since investment is exogenous. We develop the
domestic-profit alternative (the distributed rows): the household receives
$(1 - s_\pi)\Xi$ untaxed and saves the rest at the retention rate $s_\pi$,
with $s_\pi = 1$ reproducing retention exactly.

Run against the capacity door, the markup door turns a robustness check into a
result. At the fixed real wage, with the capacity door confined to the goods sectors,
every door raises the price level in every financing column except
Kaldor--Verdoorn, which lowers it in all three (the fixed-wage panels of
Tables~\ref{tab:pricing-capacity} and~\ref{tab:pricing-markup}).
Both doors move sectoral prices by the same order of magnitude. At
$\kappa = 0.5$ the markup's largest sectoral deviation (9 to 12 percent) is
two to two and a half times the capacity door's. The doors part company in the quantity block,
where the rent shows up as a leak (retention) or as additional household
demand (distribution). Whether a demand-led transformation raises or lowers
prices therefore depends on which pricing door is modelled, on which sectors
it acts and on how the programme is financed. Under external finance the
bracket runs from a 0.35 percent fall (Kaldor--Verdoorn in goods;
\texttt{pricing\_capacity-v2-GAMMA-F3-dm05-s0}) to a 1.83 percent rise
(distributed markup; \texttt{pricing\_profit-v2-GAMMA-F3-k05-spis}). Capacity
pressure (0.34 percent) and the retained markup (1.01 percent) lie in between;
the all-sector profile widens the fall to 0.82 percent
(Appendix~\ref{app:variants}). The profit share stays small --- below two
percent of factor income in every cell --- and rises with the financing
closure's demand impulse. Distributing the rent raises consumption and
employment relative to retention in every financing column of the fixed-wage
and Leontief panels, most under external finance. In the three CPI-gauge
panels of Table~\ref{tab:pricing-markup} it changes nothing: the distributed
rows repeat the retained rows exactly, because the net external transfer $F$
falls by precisely the
distributed rent (\texttt{pricing\_markup-v2-ALPHA-F2-k05} against
\texttt{pricing\_profit-v2-ALPHA-F2-k05-\{spi05,spis\}}), the same mechanism
that makes tax and external finance indistinguishable there, so the routing of
the rent matters only where the real wage is pegged. The welfare measure
changes meaning accordingly: with distributed
profit, $\Delta W$ is the consumption of a household that also owns the
firms, and each manifest carries a real-income companion (labour plus
distributed profit, deflated by the CPI) beside it. Two caveats bound the
reading: the markup is symmetric about the baseline, so a contracting sector
prices at a discount ($\mu_i < 1$; the run records the range), and leaving the
rent untaxed understates the fiscal response under tax finance.
