\section*{Reviewer 2}

\subsection*{R2.0 --- General assessment: the bridge, and what the paper now
measures}
\refcom{The paper compares the effects of an exogenous increase in final demand for
a specified range of goods and services (meant to reflect required real
investment purchases associated with a transformation of the German housing
sector towards carbon neutrality) across two alternative analytic frameworks
-- a traditional fixed-price input-output (IO) multiplier model with
unlimited primary factor supply and Leontief (fixed input coefficient)
technologies, and a stylized computable general equilibrium (CGE) model with
primary factor supply constraints which allows for substitution among inputs
on the production side and substitution between commodities on the final
demand side.

Unsurprisingly, the IO analysis suggests a positive real GDP effect as
primary factor employment rises in response to the imposed final demand
increase, whereas in the CGE analysis the real GDP effect is virtually nil
as primary factor supplies remain by assumption fixed at pre-shock level
(Figure 1).

Also unsurprisingly, when the CGE analysis is modified by simulating the
imposed final demand increase together with a simultaneous exogenous
increase in factor supply of the same magnitude as occurring in the IO
model, the CGE model generates a real GDP expansion of the same order of
magnitude as the IO model (Figure 4). And -- as a matter of course -- when
the CGE analysis is further modified by simulating the imposed final demand
increase together with a simultaneous exogenous increase in factor supply of
a larger magnitude than occurring in the IO model, the CGE model generates a
real GDP expansion larger than the GDP effect suggested by the IO model
(Figure 7).

No substantive novel insights emerge from this exercise.

The inquiry is framed as a search for `commensurability' between the two
approaches: `we aim to trace the most relevant single assumptions leading to
differences in obtained estimates, explore their relative impact on the
results and thereby illuminate a concrete case of a paradigmatic cleavage to
better understand the possibilities and constraints for building bridges
across rivers of paradigmatic confinement' (p.3). By `bridges' the authors
mean changes in assumptions (in their terminology `axiomatic variations') in
the CGE model, that would generate results that are closer to the results of
the IO multiplier analysis.

These `axiomatic variations' comprise (i) reductions in three sets of
substitution elasticities (between commodities in final demand, between
commodities in intermediate input demand, and between composite intermediate
and the single primary production factor labour), and (ii) a relaxation of
the labour supply constraint(s).

Thus, the `bridges across rivers of paradigmatic confinement' the authors
seek to build in this paper already exist. Hence, the present paper is
superfluous and fails to provide any novel or interesting insights.

The authors' astonishing failure to engage with the existing literature on
alternative factor market closures in CGE models is the opposite of
professional scholarship. I cannot recommend the paper for publication, and
my collegial advice to the authors would be to withdraw it from the public
domain to avoid attracting further ridicule of the type I was unable to
suppress in my comments (despite trying hard).

\textit{Summing up:} This paper is not exactly a masterpiece of excellent scholarship. Some
casual engagement with the existing related literature would have shown that
the authors' bridgebuilding exercise between IO multiplier analysis and CGE
analysis is unnecessary: The bridge already exists. Technically, it is a
fairly trivial task to transform a standard CGE model into a set-up that
generates results akin to the traditional IO model (thus turning a general
model with a wider domain of applicability into a special case with a narrow
domain of applicability). The authors' cack-handed approach to the matter
obscures rather than illuminates the issue. No novel or interesting insights
emerge from this analysis.}
\response We concede the central point of this report without reservation. The bridge
exists: the input-output multiplier model is a special case of a CGE model
with fixed prices and unconstrained primary factor supply
\citep{Robinson.2006}, a relation long established in the closure literature
\citep{Rose.1995, Dervis.1982}, and the elasticities of primary factor supply
-- in the one-factor world of this paper, the elasticity of labor supply --
are what determine how far a model travels along it \citep{deMeloTarr.1992}.
Our original submission presented the search for that bridge as its
contribution, and the referee is right that this could not yield a novel
insight. We have withdrawn the bridge-discovery framing: the revised
manuscript states the relation up front and treats it as known rather than as
a finding.

Two consequences of this report are now built into the model rather than
argued away. The elastic labor supply the report recommends in place of our
exogenous injection of slack is a closure in its own right, with the supply
elasticity as an explicit dimension of the comparison rather than a scenario
device. And the alternative factor-market specification the report recommends
-- labor as an intersectorally mobile factor at a uniform economy-wide wage
-- is now one of the five labor-market closures the paper compares, set
alongside the sector-specific variants rather than displaced by them.

What the paper claims instead is the measurement of the space this bridge
crosses: five labor-market closures crossed with three financing regimes,
applied to one empirically calibrated green-investment impulse. The
measurement is the contribution, and its result is that the closure does not
move everything: which sectors gain and which lose is largely fixed by the
input-output structure and the composition of the program, and that ranking
survives every closure; but whether the expansion of the program sectors
shows up in quantities or in prices, how large the aggregate response is, and
whether the rest of the economy contracts or expands with it, is decided by
the closure. That, rather than a new bridge, is what we offer applied research
on the socio-ecological transformation.

The report's specific objections are answered point by point below; several of
them -- the sign error in the cost equation, the supply-and-demand
characterization, the CES novelty claim and the Cobb-Douglas ``archetype'' --
are conceded outright.

\subsection*{R2.1 --- Labor-supply elasticity as the bridging parameter}
\refcom{The authors find it `curious' (p.17) that `axiomatic variation' (i) in
isolation does not reduce the `paradigmatic distance in terms of results'
(p.17/18) (i.e. the difference between the real GDP effects generated by the
two models). In fact, the only `curious' aspect of this trivial (in the
sense of: a priory obvious) finding is that it takes the authors some 17
pages to arrive at this insight: There are essentially three sources of GDP
growth, namely primary factor employment growth, factor productivity growth,
and improvements in the efficiency of factor allocation. As the factor
productivity parameters are not affected by the simulated demand shock and
the presence of distortionary taxes (that entail allocative inefficiencies
by driving wedges between relative producer prices and the marginal rates of
transformation) are swept under the carpet in the authors' stylized CGE
model, it is a priory evident that only `axiomatic variation' (ii) can
generate an expansionary aggregate real GDP response to the imposed demand
shock.

The authors eventually arrive at the same conclusion, but are --
understandably -- not quite satisfied with their own approach of achieving
the desired GDP increase only via an exogenous ad hoc injection of
additional labour as a separate shock on top of the imposed final demand
shock:

`\dots{} variations in the imposed elasticity have little to no impact on
final results for large parts of the relevant parameter space. The second
variation-- the introduction of labor slack-- on the other hand is useful
for creating outcomes that align more closely with those achieved by the
traditional Leontief approach. However, the assumption of positive labor
slack in this context plays the role of a deus ex machina that implicitly
governs the GDP-expansion achieved as increases in input map roughly
one-to-one on increases in aggregate output. Hence, on this level a
fundamental cleavage in terms of assumptions remains as the Leontief model
conceives new equilibria as essentially demand-driven, which is a feature
that cannot be reproduced in a general equilibrium context by simply adding
slack on factor markets' (p.23/24).

What the authors fail to see here is that there is an obvious
straightforward alternative to the authors' clumsy way of introducing labour
slack into the CGE via exogenous injections of labour -- namely simply by
endogenizing the labour supply within the model, or , stated equivalently,
by replacing the assumption of a completely inelastic labour supply with the
assumption of a finitely elastic or infinitely elastic labour supply with
respect to the real wage.

In a sense, the authors' intuition that there is some set of elasticities
for which an `axiomatic variation' of parameter values would bring the
results of a CGE demand shock analysis with fixed primary factor supplies
closer in line with that of an old-fashioned IO multiplier analysis is
entirely correct. But -- tragically and comically at the same time -- the
paper fails to identify the one obvious set of elasticities that really
matters for this line of inquiry: the elasticities of primary factor supply
(which boils down to the labour supply elasticity in the paper's one-factor
world). Along the continuum from zero (as in the paper's CGE model) to
infinity (as in the IO multiplier model) it is this elasticity value that
governs how far one travels along the `bridge'. It is as simple as that.}

\subsection*{R2.2 --- Closure literature}
\refcom{Such specifications are readily available in the existing CGE literature. In
some models finitely elastic labour supply functions are explicitly derived
from a model of optimal labour-leisure choice (see e.g. de Melo and Tarr,
1992: 112ff) to provide a rigorous neoclassical microfoundation for this
specification, while other models impose labour supply function in ad hoc
fashion -- i.e. without entering leisure as an argument in the utility
functions of households. Labour market closures with infinitely elastic
supply for at least some types of labour are frequently used in CGE models
for developing countries in the tradition of Dervis, de Melo Robinson
(1982). A reasonable standard procedure in such models is to assume
unlimited supply of unskilled labour while treating skilled labour as
supply-constrained. Technically, such models are particularly easy to
implement -- a model with fixed labour supplies can turned into a model with
unlimited supply of labour within seconds by fixing the wage rate at
baseline level and by dropping the labour supply constraint(s). In the
one-factor CGE model considered in the present paper, the model becomes
`essentially demand-driven' under this factor market closure.

As Robinson (2006: 2016) puts it succinctly, `it is possible to make a CGE
model behave like a fixed-price multiplier model by assuming that primary
input prices are all fixed and that supplies of primary inputs are
unconstrained'.}
\response{The reviewer is right about the construction, and we now say so explicitly
rather than leaving it implicit. Fixing primary input prices and dropping the
supply constraint is exactly the closure our fourth column runs, and it reproduces
the Leontief-inverse multiplier exactly at every value of the substitution
elasticities (the employment-adjustment equivalence in Appendix~B); we now acknowledge that this closure is in the lineage of Dervis, de Melo and Robinson (1982) and de Melo and Tarr (1992), which we now cite where it belongs. We also explicitly introduce the elastic labor supply closure in its sectoral form and explicitly discuss overlaps between different closure formulation in Appendix~A. What we deemed to be beyond the scope of this paper is the introduction of different skill levels, which does not align naturally with considerations on transformational issues (which often do not lack skill per se, but priority and repurposing in terms of financing).}

\subsection*{R2.3 --- The 'paradigmatic cleavage' framing}
\refcom{The paper's characterisation of a `paradigmatic cleavage' between
traditional IO analysis and CGE analysis is contrived and falls flat in
several respects (For a more perceptive and informative discussion of the
relation between IO and CGE analysis -- written more than three decades ago
-- see Rose (1995) and references therein).

In particular, the framing of such a cleavage as an issue about the Leontief
inverse (`the first option is represented by the classic Leontief-Inverse'
(p.2)\dots{} `whereas the canonical computable general equilibrium (CGE)
models represent an alternative vision' (p.3); `2.1 A 'Keynesian' approach:
The Leontief-Inverse' (p.5) ; `2.2 A 'classical' approach: Computable
General Equilibrium') is plainly absurd. No CGE modeller has any problem
with the Leontief inverse as such. (Indeed, most existing CGE models
maintain the assumption of a Leontief technology with fixed input-output
coefficients for intermediate inputs - the $\epsilon$=0 case in the present
paper's notation -- and set $\theta$ to zero or a low positive value, while
allowing for substitutability between primary factors of production -- which
does not figure at all in the single-factor world of the present paper). As
a matter of course, the Leontief inverse provides useful information about
backward linkages among sectors. This referee, for instance, routinely uses
the Leontief inverse in CGE courses as a means to explain the sectoral
output responses to an exogenous shock.

From a history-of-economic-thought perspective, the development of the
modern CGE approach has in part been motivated by -- and can be rationally
reconstructed as a response to -- dissatisfaction with the limitations of
the traditional input-output model as a tool for multisectoral applied
economic policy analysis (see e.g. Robinson (1989), Willenbockel (1994:
44)). This response involved the relaxation of the extreme linearity and
factor abundance assumptions of the IO model. If one rolls back these
relaxations, one arrives back at the original IO model. In other words, and
as already pointed out in the general comments above, the original IO model
can be seen as a special case of a CGE model. From this perspective, the
bridges between the two are already there in plain sight -- and thus the
search for such bridges cannot possibly considered as fruitful line of
inquiry that would advance the current state of knowledge.

From a pragmatic applied policy modelling perspective, it appears in my view
more fruitful to look at the two approaches from a `domain of applicability'
angle rather than from a Kuhnian `paradigmatic cleavage' angle: The domain
of applicability of the basic IO multiplier model is strictly speaking
restricted to a severe recession situation with pervasive involuntary
unemployment and pervasive underutilization of existing productive capital,
while the domain of applicability of the authors' CGE model with its
completely inelastic primary factor supply assumption is strictly speaking a
boom situation with full utilization of resources, but as already noted in
my general comments the domain of applicability of the CGE can be extended
by allowing for elastic factor supplies.

Thus, as a case in point, in a nutshell the current economic situation of
Germany can be loosely characterized by a simultaneous presence of
unemployment primarily for low- and unskilled labor and shortages for
certain types of skilled labor.}

\subsection*{R2.14 --- Units of the reported price changes}
\refcom{[the referee's wording on the normalisation of reported price changes ---

\response{We thank the referee: this was wrong, and chasing it turned up a second,
more substantive error of ours. The two are related but distinct, and we have
treated them as such.

\emph{Units.} The pricing tables compared nominal magnitudes expressed in two
different units. The flexible-wage closures carried the consumption price index as
numeraire, so their rows were already in consumption units; the wage-pinned rows
fixed the money wage instead, which left prices in wage units. In sharp contrast, the revised manuscript reports consumption units for all models: prices are divided by the cell's own CPI, the GDP deflator is rescaled with them, and the maximum sectoral deviation is computed on the normalised price vector. 

It remains to be added that the CPI sits at one as long as no prices move and, hence, plays a role mainly for the no-reallocation and the elastic labor supply closure as well as the extensions presented in Section~6.

\emph{Closure.} The reason the CPI moved at all is that the closure we had called
a ``fixed real wage'' closure does not peg the real wage. It fixes the money wage
as the numeraire, sets the net external transfer to zero and drops the
labour-supply equation, so that employment adjusts. With one production factor
and constant returns the real wage is not an instrument at all: it is fixed by
technology, $w/\mathcal{P}(p) = 1/\mathcal{P}(\pi(A))$, so a peg on it is
redundant at the calibration point and infeasible away from it (the
no-real-wage-peg corollary in Appendix~B). We have renamed the closure
\emph{employment adjustment}, stated its equations exactly as they are solved,
and added the identity with its proof. No estimate in the paper changes: the
kernel never solved the peg, and real outcomes are invariant to the nominal
scale. What changes is the label, the interpretation --- in particular we no
longer claim that workers' purchasing power is held fixed there --- and the
units.

The two corrections cannot substitute for one another, which is why we report
both: renormalising cannot restore a constant real wage, and relabelling cannot
repair mixed units. Reporting the wage-pinned rows in consumption units is in
fact what makes the movement of the real wage visible instead of hidden inside
the reporting convention.}
