\section{Two primitive questions, a simplified matrix and some complications}\label{sec:two-questions}

As already indicated, key points of disjuncture in input-output modeling can be mapped onto classic cleavages separating different traditions of economic thought. Such traditions can often be characterized in terms of the basic analytical ideas -- or ``rivers'' in the history of economic thought \citep[][p. 9]{Screpanti2005} -- they endorse. Where these basic ideas have a different emphasis or contradict outright, a paradigmatic cleavage is in sight \citep{Dobusch.2012}. Two such classic fields of tension in economic thinking also lie at the heart of different approaches to input-output modeling: the nature of the short run and the role of money and credit, as already captured by Table~\ref{tab:overview}.

When mapping the rough distinctions from our introductory table more specifically to the realm of input-output models, we find that different labor market closures are the key entry point for different understandings of the short run: if production factors are fully utilized, increased monetary demand will shift wages and prices as capacity constraints bind. While this view is traditionally associated with computable general equilibrium models (CGEs), the core intution does not hold for models with just one production factor. Here, relative scarcity does not kick in so that prices and wages stay constant and demand is simply reallocated to other sectors –– the underlying trade-off materializes in a change in the composition of output but does not affect prices. However, as shown below with the example of the ``no reallocation'' model, prices do respond even in a one factor model when capacity constraints bind on a sectoral, instead of aggregate, level.

Conversely, if free factors of production are available, additional expenditures will lead to an expansion of employment and, correspondingly, output. This classic output adjustment closure \citep{Taylor1990} is what one gets from an application of the Leontief inverse. Both visions can be unified in one analytical apparatus (see \citealp{Robinson.2006} and Section~\ref{sec:kernel}), just differentiated by different assumptions on what is exogenous or endogenous when monetary expenditures increase. The two poles are the standard CGE closure, in which one flexible wage clears the labor market at full employment and additional demand is met by reallocation at given aggregate output, and the employment-adjustment closure, in which a pinned money wage and constant costs set prices so that employment and output absorb demand. Under demand-only shocks the latter reproduces the Leontief-inverse multiplier exactly, and it does so at every value of the substitution elasticities; its fixed-coefficient corner, the Leontief closure, therefore yields the same result (Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}). While the former is the obvious neoclassical standard model \citep[see][]{Sen1963, TaylorLysy1979}, the latter reflects the intuition of a multiplier and a notion of economic planning that shows greater resonance with (Post-)Keynesian, radical and evolutionary approaches \citep{Pollin.2015,Mazzucato.2018}. The division is older than our labels: the postwar synthesis posed it at the
aggregate level, with the horizontal and vertical segments of the aggregate
supply curve standing for the two poles \citep{Hicks.1937,Modigliani.1944};
what our two axes disaggregate is which of them an applied model has
implicitly chosen.

Financial closures on the other hand encode prior beliefs about the role of our monetary system and the nature of money. As input-output models are typically treated as static, operating outside of time \citep[see however][]{Mercure.2018,Rocchi.2025,McGregor.1996}, there is no borrowing against the future as this would be equivalent to a positive leakage. Hence, additional expenditure cannot be financed domestically in the conventional sense without threatening the internal consistency of the model, where additional expenditure has to be refinanced within the same period (see however \citealp{Duchin.1998}). Hence, while a traditional, simplified application of a Leontief inverse would suggest some external stimulus, the internal consistency of the model requires us to specify where that stimulus comes from. This implies that input-output models in their canonical interpretation are difficult to align with ideas on endogenous money creation as they lacks any framework to conceptualize the intertemporal role of debt \citep{Lavoie.2022}. As a practical workaround the economy can be assumed to take on foreign debt, which simulates an exogenous inflow, in a less invasive way that does not threaten the internal consistency of the model.

While the crosswise recombination of these different closure options already gives rise to some complexity, the underlying choice menu is nonetheless somewhat rough and -- in the case of the short-run -- simplistically dichotomous. Hence, it comes as no surprise that further established variations exist that try to address this point. An obvious starting point from a mainstream perspective to introduce greater variety in labor market outcomes is by explicitly referring to a leisure-income trade-off, which leads to the canonical \textit{elastic labor supply model} \citep[see][112ff]{deMeloTarr.1992}, % AG (Oct 2026): Dervis.1982 added here so that the letter's lineage claim (R2.2) holds in the text.
a closure with a long lineage in applied multisectoral models \citep[see][]{Dervis.1982}: Assuming that the substitution effect dominates, higher wages will attract additional labor so that capacity constraints do not bite with full force. In the \emph{elastic labor supply} closure the fixed labor endowment is replaced by a sector-specific labor supply curve, $L_i^{s} = \bar L_i\,[\,(w_i/\mathcal{P}(p))/(w_{i0}/\mathcal{P}_{0})\,]^{\eta_s}$, with the elasticities set to one common value, $\eta_s \equiv \eta_s$. Two changes of functional form follow: labor supply becomes \emph{behavioral} rather than a quantity restriction or a price pin, and the single aggregate labor market becomes $N$ sectoral markets with $N$ wages.\footnote{The latter assumption is not strictly necessary: a single-wage form, with one aggregate supply curve $\sum_i L_i = \bar L\,[\,(w/\mathcal{P}(p))/(w_{0}/\mathcal{P}_{0})\,]^{\eta_s}$, is also possible. Under demand-only shocks, however, this single-wage form coincides with the standard CGE closure for every $\eta_s$ (Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}), so the sectoral form is what makes the elasticity matter in our case of application, which typically implies shocks with rather heterogeneous impact on sectors.}

While this modification brings greater responsiveness on the side of employment other variations in the mainstream literature assume forms of rigidity that go even beyond the conventional CGE labor market closure. A prominent recent example is given by \citet{BF2019}, who introduce a ``no-reallocation'' case as a baseline scenario to articulate the view that sectoral productivity shocks might impact on aggregate outcomes in a non-linear way. Relative to Table~\ref{tab:closure-conditions} this is not a column but
a more rigid, sectoral extension of the labor axis: it projects the standard CGE column's aggregate restriction on a sectoral level, i.e., $ L_i = \bar L_i$ (fixed employment levels for all sectors, so ``no-reallocation'') and correspondingly relies on sectorally flexible wages $w_i$. Formally, the transition from the standard CGE model to the no-reallocation model is governed by $\eta$, which represents the mobility of labor across sectors and admits two cases in our setup: $\eta=0$ for the no-reallocation case and $\eta=1$ for a full reallocation case that coincides with the standard CGE model.

In the context of our case of application -- a socio-ecological transformation that is tied to sector-specific shifts or injections in final demand -- such a baseline setup is most pessimistic for financial closures F2 and F3, as it assumes labor market rigidity across sectors, which implies that capacity constraints bite quickly and prices react accordingly. As an effect, sectors with strong demand shocks are then overwhelmed as workers from other sectors cannot ``reallocate'' to where capacity is needed. This result, however, resonates with intuition that additional rigidities on labor markets will complicate transformation efforts building on exactly such a reallocation of labor.\footnote{In this case assumptions deviate from the neoclassical baseline, but strengthen the general laissez-faire policy stance associated with its standard closure. Also note that related work on rigidities in production, capital or financial markets also exists, but is beyond the scope of this paper \citep[see, however, ][]{BF2022, BernankeGertlerGilchrist.1999}.}

Technically the \emph{Leontief closure} named here can be represented with the very same analytical apparatus as the conventional CGE model: with substitution elasticities taken to their Leontief limits \citep[see][]{Leontief.1936}, fully flexible production factors and a wage that is pinned rather than market-cleared \eqref{eq:closure-delta}, prices then follow from constant costs and final demand determines output. This is the corner most applied transformation studies work with, usually without emphasis. Given this restrictive setup the \emph{Leontief closure} can itself be conceived as corner solution of a broader set of related approaches that retain the basic idea that ``quantities adjust'' without also committing to a Leontief production function -- although under demand-only shocks this corner is not distinct from the rest of the set: the employment-adjustment closure yields the identical result at every value of the substitution elasticities, and the rigidity of production starts to matter only once supply shocks enter (Section~\ref{sec:beyond-demand-only}). In their basic form these \textit{wage-pinned} models hold the money wage at its baseline value, $w=1$ \eqref{eq:closure-gamma}, which pins down prices and leaves the quantity margin free; the real wage is then an outcome rather than a restriction, since technology fixes it (Corollary~\ref{cor:realwage}). They stand in the same tradition as the Leontief inverse: it is the (more general) closure through which the Keynesian multiplier intuition enters the kernel (see also section \ref{sec:kernel}).

Observing these additional models and corresponding closure variations moves our view on the short run from the Manichaean scenario formulated in table \ref{tab:closure-conditions} to a more nuanced exposition of different conceptions of the short run: the \textit{no-reallocation approach} imposes additional rigidity on labor markets, \textit{elastic labor supply models} allow for introducing behavioral responses and the \textit{employment-adjustment models} try to generalize the scope of the output adjustment view. Moreover, the first two of these approaches are articulated on a sectoral level and the latter can be extended to one. In what follows we suggest to take the now resulting portfolio of closure choices as means to illustrate the consequences of different closure assumptions -- and, hence, paradigmatic perspectives -- on results when studying the impacts of a socio-ecological transformation by means of input-output models.

Before we map these five closures in a monumental table \ref{tab:closure-matrix-3x5} further below, it makes sense to point out the sometimes surprising overlaps that arise for certain characteristic parameter values under demand-only shocks -- the case of our application -- as summarized by Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} below. For one, the no-reallocation model differs from the conventional CGE model only by exactly this ``no-reallocation'' assumption, whereas choosing a reallocation term $\eta=1$ instead of $\eta=0$ would lead to full convergence with the conventional case (as the sectoral level ceases to matter with ``full reallocation''). For another, the elastic labor supply model -- being a curve of solutions rather than a simple corner -- reproduces the no-reallocation model exactly when labor market participants lack any flexibility ($\eta_s=0$), and approaches the \textit{employment-adjustment} closure at the inverse position ($\eta_s \rightarrow \infty$). However, that approach is a limit rather than an intersection: prices and the
wage anchor agree, the net external transfer does not --- it adjusts in the elastic-supply model and is fixed at zero by the employment-adjustment closure --- so the two allocations differ by the transfer's contribution to demand (see Appendices \ref{app:equivalences}, \ref{app:closure-systems} and, especially, \ref{app:sectoral} for further details on this aspect).

Hence, the elastic labor supply model can be said to sit at the center of the interparadigmatic bridge between neoclassical interpretations based on factor scarcity and more Keynesian multiplier-style interpretations based on extrapolation via the Leontief inverse \citep{Robinson.2006, Taylor1990}.\footnote{Also, under demand-only shocks the elastic labor supply model collapses to the conventional CGE in its single-wage form, i.e.\ in the absence of sectoral wage heterogeneity, for every value of $\eta_s$.} 
Finally, as already indicated, under demand-only shocks the \textit{employment-adjustment} closure is equivalent to the classic Leontief result whatever the substitution elasticities, so that the rigidity of production structures matters only once supply shocks enter. A more detailed exposition drawing on the basic framework as established in section \ref{sec:kernel} that rationalizes the overlaps between different closures is found in Appendix~\ref{app:equivalences}.

Based on this core observation that a series of, partially overlapping, key axiomatic variations \citep{Kapeller.2013} exist when it comes to closure choice in input-output modeling we aim to map the basic framework as sketched in Figure \ref{fig:flexibility-map} to an applied level by asking: which of these assumptions will typically make what difference for estimating the economic effects of transformational policies. Moreover, as estimates for such economic effects often serve as a baseline for and precondition for estimating further ecological effects and also play a key role in informing political decision-making, the relevance of this question reaches far beyond the mere technical level. Following this agenda we combine the estimate of investment requirements introduced in section \ref{sec:io-bridge} across different model specifications to better reflect on how the conceptual distinctions introduced above matter for applied analysis and how different closure dimensions (here: labor markets and financing) interact in practice.
