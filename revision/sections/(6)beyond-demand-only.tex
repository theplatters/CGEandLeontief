\subsection{The pricing door I: capacity and Kaldor--Verdoorn}\label{subsec:pricing-capacity}
% Provenance: ADR-0029; runs pricing_capacity-v1-GAMMA-* and
% pricing_mobile-v1-ALPHA-*-d{p,m}05 (OpenCGE v0.2.0); Table~\ref{tab:pricing-door}
% and Figure~\ref{fig:pricing-bracket} are generated by
% revision/tables/make_pricing_door.py. Numbers below are cited by run id.
The equivalences of Section~\ref{subsec:equivalences} are not properties of the
labour market. They are properties of the price block. Under a demand-only shock
($A \equiv 1$) the zero-profit conditions are homogeneous of degree one in prices
and wages and contain no demand term, so they pin relative prices at their
baseline whatever the labour or financing closure (Lemma~\ref{lem:price}); the
quantity block then reduces to an affine multiplier system. Every closure the
matrix distinguishes therefore acts on quantities only, and the one argument a
demand shock cannot reach is the unit cost itself.

The unit cost has exactly three arguments a demand shock could move: technology,
the wage and the price of intermediate inputs. Sectoral wages are the labour
door of Section~\ref{subsec:sectoral-family}. The first \emph{pricing door} lets
effective technology respond to utilisation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capacity-door}
  A^{\mathrm{eff}}_i \;=\; A_i\,\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i}\Bigr)^{-\delta},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_i$ baseline gross output, so that $\delta = 0$ is the model of the
preceding sections exactly. The elasticity $\delta$ is a reduced-form
utilisation elasticity, not a rental rate: no factor is added and no income
block is created, because the productivity change passes into unit cost under
zero profit. Its two signs carry two traditions. With $\delta > 0$ unit cost
rises with own output --- capacity pressure, the short-run cost curve of a
plant run beyond its design load. With $\delta < 0$ unit cost falls with
output --- the dynamic increasing returns of \textcite{Verdoorn1949} and
\textcite{Kaldor1966}, which \textcite{SylosLabini1995} reads into the very
production functions this kernel uses. The door is solved inside the
equilibrium residual, not iterated around it; because $\delta < 0$ is a
positive feedback, every priced cell carries a uniqueness gate (several
starting points must reach the same solution, and the residual Jacobian must
have full rank; Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-door}).

Panel A of Table~\ref{tab:pricing-door} reports the door under the programme
of Section~\ref{sec:application} at the fixed real wage. Its first result is
that the door works: with $\delta = \pm 0.5$ the largest sectoral price
deviation lies between 9.8 and 12.9 percent in every financing column, where
the fixed-wage column of Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} reports none. Its second result is that the \emph{sign} of the price-level
response is not a property of the door. Kaldor--Verdoorn returns lower the GDP
deflator only under external finance --- by 1.47 percent
(\texttt{pricing\_capacity-v1-GAMMA-F3-dm05}) --- and raise it slightly under
preference reallocation and tax finance
(\texttt{pricing\_capacity-v1-GAMMA-F1-dm05}, \texttt{-F2-dm05}). ``Verdoorn
lowers prices'' is an F3 statement: only additional, externally financed
demand expands enough sectors far enough for the productivity gains to
dominate. One reading, which the aggregate numbers suggest but do not
establish: a recomposition or a tax-financed substitution of demand contracts
some sectors while it expands others, and the contracting sectors lose
productivity under the same elasticity. Under the CPI numeraire
(panel D) the same door moves relative prices and the real wage instead, and
F2 and F3 remain indistinguishable: financing neutrality in the mobile gauge
survives the door.

Two consequences follow, and both are statements about scope rather than new
numbers. The fixed-wage and Leontief columns coincide in
Table~\ref{tab:results-matrix} because both hold prices at the baseline; once
the door moves prices, substitution elasticities matter again and the
coincidence dissolves. Likewise, elastic labour supply differs from the
standard CGE closure only through the real wage, which the demand-only matrix
pins; a pricing door releases it. The equivalences of
Section~\ref{subsec:equivalences} therefore hold on the slice ``$A \equiv 1$
\emph{and} price-invariant technology'', and every claim built on them carries
that clause.

\subsection{The pricing door II: the distribution markup}\label{subsec:pricing-markup}
% Provenance: ADR-0029 (Stage A), ADR-0030 (Stage B); runs
% pricing_markup-v1-GAMMA-*, pricing_profit-v1-GAMMA-*,
% pricing_mobile-v1-ALPHA-*-k*.
The capacity door creates no rent. The second door does: a markup that rises
with utilisation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:markup-door}
  p_i \;=\; \mu_i\,c_i, \qquad \mu_i \;=\; 1 + \kappa\Bigl(\frac{y_i}{\lambda_i} - 1\Bigr),
\end{equation}
nested at $\kappa = 0$. Pricing power that moves with demand has a long
pedigree, from the degree of monopoly of \textcite{Kalecki1954} to the
procyclical marginal cost of \textcite{Bils1987}, and once the rent is paid
out it opens the wage-led versus profit-led question of
\textcite{BhaduriMarglin1990}. This section uses it only as a pricing
closure, not as a theory of distribution. Two modelling choices make
it a closure. First, firms still hire labour and inputs at \emph{marginal
cost} $c_i = p_i/\mu_i$, while households and other firms buy at $p_i$; the
four places where the price enters a first-order condition are re-derived
accordingly in Appendix~\ref{app:pricing-door}. Second, the rent
$\Pi = \sum_i (1 - 1/\mu_i)\,p_i y_i$ must go somewhere.

Table~\ref{tab:routing} lists the four places it can go. Foreign owners would
book it in the external account; a government wedge would make the state a
third financing instrument beside F2 and F3. We take \emph{retention} as the
baseline (panel B of Table~\ref{tab:pricing-door}): the rent is booked as
saving by firms, the household budget is unchanged and all real effects run
through relative prices. Retention is the cheapest closure that keeps the
external-account identity exact; its cost, stated rather than implied, is that
retained funds are hoarded, since investment is exogenous. We develop the
domestic-profit alternative (panel C): the household receives
$(1 - s_\pi)\Pi$ untaxed and saves the rest at the retention rate $s_\pi$,
with $s_\pi = 1$ reproducing retention exactly.

Run against the capacity door, the markup door turns a robustness check into a
result. At the fixed real wage every door raises the price level in every
financing column, with
one exception --- Kaldor--Verdoorn under external finance --- and both doors
move sectoral prices by similar orders of magnitude
(Figure~\ref{fig:pricing-bracket}); they part company in the quantity block, where the rent shows up as a leak (retention)
or as additional household demand (distribution). Whether a demand-led
transformation raises or lowers prices therefore depends on which pricing door
is modelled and on how the programme is financed: under external finance the
bracket runs from a 1.47 percent fall (Kaldor--Verdoorn) to a 2.15 percent
rise (capacity pressure; \texttt{pricing\_capacity-v1-GAMMA-F3-dp05}), with
the markup doors in between. The profit share stays small --- below two
percent of factor income in every cell --- and rises with the financing
closure's demand impulse. Distributing the rent raises consumption and
employment relative to retention in every column, most under external
finance. The welfare measure changes meaning accordingly: with distributed
profit, $\Delta W$ is the consumption of a household that also owns the
firms, and each manifest carries a real-income companion (labour plus
distributed profit, deflated by the CPI) beside it. Two caveats bound the
reading: the markup is symmetric about the baseline, so a contracting sector
prices at a discount ($\mu_i < 1$; the run records the range), and leaving the
rent untaxed understates the fiscal response under tax finance.
